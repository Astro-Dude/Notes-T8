1 Risk, return & diversification

Explain like I'm 5

On Juhu beach, the umbrella seller earns when it rains and the ice-cream seller earns when it's sunny. Own a share of both stalls and you earn something every day — that's diversification.

  • Portfolio = a collection of assets. Weight wi = rupees in asset i ÷ total rupees (weights add to 1).
  • Expected return E(R) and risk σ (standard deviation) of a single asset — as in Session 12: E(R) = Σ ps·Rs, σ = √Σ ps(Rs − E(R))2.
  • Portfolio return is always the weighted average of the assets' returns.
  • Portfolio risk is less than the weighted average of the σs whenever assets don't move in perfect lock-step (correlation < +1). This free reduction in risk is diversification.
  • Modern Portfolio Theory (Harry Markowitz, 1952): judge an asset by what it does to the portfolio's risk, not by its own σ.

  • "10 stocks = diversified." Not if all 10 are banks — their correlation is high, so σ barely falls. Diversify across sectors and asset classes (equity, debt, gold).
  • Thinking diversification removes all risk. It removes only unsystematic risk.
Recap Return averages, risk doesn't. Adding assets that aren't perfectly correlated cuts unsystematic risk; systematic risk stays.

2 Two-asset portfolio: covariance, correlation & risk

Explain like I'm 5

If your two friends always laugh at the same jokes, inviting both doesn't make the party less dull on a bad night. If one laughs when the other doesn't, the mood evens out. Correlation measures that "laughing together".

Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the 2 in 2·wA·wB·Cov, or forgetting to square the weights.
  • Mixing units: σ = 30% → use 30 (then σ2 = 900 %2) or 0.30 (σ2 = 0.09) — never 30 with 0.09.
  • Putting ρ where Cov belongs. Cov = ρσAσB; ρ = 0.3 is not a covariance.
  • Taking the square root too early: add all three terms first, then √.
Recap E(Rp) is a weighted average; σp needs the covariance term. Lower ρ → lower σp at the same expected return.

3 Minimum-variance portfolio & efficient frontier

Explain like I'm 5

Mixing two masalas: some blends are both tastier and less spicy than others. The efficient frontier is the list of blends where you can't get more taste without more spice.

Minimum-variance weightwA* = (σB2 − Cov(A,B)) / (σA2 + σB2 − 2·Cov(A,B))   ·   wB* = 1 − wA*
  • Special case ρ = −1: wA* = σB / (σA + σB) → σp = 0.
  • Special case ρ = 0: wA* = σB2 / (σA2 + σB2).
Worked example — minimum-variance mix of Ganga (A) and Kaveri (B), ρ = 0.3

Given: σA = 30%, σB = 20%, ρ = 0.3, E(RA) = 16%, E(RB) = 10%.

  1. Cov = 0.3 × 30 × 20 = 180 (%2)
  2. wA* = (400 − 180) / (900 + 400 − 2 × 180) = 220 / 940 = 0.2340 → wB* = 0.7660
  3. E(Rp) = 0.2340 × 16 + 0.7660 × 10 = 11.40%
  4. σp = √(0.23402 × 900 + 0.76602 × 400 + 2 × 0.2340 × 0.7660 × 180) = 18.67%

Interpretation: putting 23.4% (₹2,34,043 of ₹10 lakh) into the riskier stock gives lower risk (18.67%) than holding only the "safe" stock B (20%) — and more return.

Same pair, other correlations: ρ = −1 → wA* = 20/50 = 0.40, σp = 0, E(Rp) = 12.4% (a riskless combo). ρ = 0 → wA* = 400/1,300 = 0.3077, σp = 16.64%.

  • With many assets the same idea gives a curved efficient frontier: the set of portfolios with the highest return for each level of risk (or lowest risk for each return).
  • Each investor picks a point on it by risk appetite. With a risk-free asset added, the choice simplifies further (§4).

Markowitz assumptions

  • Investors are rational and risk-averse: for the same return they prefer less risk; for the same risk, more return.
  • Decisions depend only on expected return and variance (returns roughly normal).
  • Single holding period; investors can estimate E(R), σ and ρ for every asset.
  • No taxes or transaction costs; assets are infinitely divisible.

Limits in practice: estimates of E(R) and ρ are noisy, so "optimal" weights jump around; correlations rise in crashes (2008, March 2020), exactly when diversification is needed most.

Recap wA* = (σB2 − Cov)/(σA2 + σB2 − 2Cov). Mixes above the MVP are efficient; below it are dominated.

4 Sharpe ratio & the Capital Market Line

Explain like I'm 5

Two auto drivers both reach Andheri; one drove smoothly, one braked hard all the way. Sharpe ratio rewards the trip per unit of jerk — extra return per unit of risk.

Sharpe ratioSharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp
  • Rp − Rf = excess return over a risk-free asset (illustrative Rf = 7%, 10-year G-Sec). σp = total risk.
  • Higher Sharpe = better reward per unit of total risk. Use it to compare funds or portfolios.
  • Cousin: Treynor ratio = (Rp − Rf) / β — uses only systematic risk; fits well-diversified portfolios.
Worked example — which fund would you pick?

Illustrative 5-year averages, Rf = 7%.

FundReturnσExcess returnSharpeRank
X (flexi-cap)15%20%8%0.402
Y (balanced)12%10%5%0.501
Z (small-cap)18%28%11%0.393
Nifty 50 index fund12%15%5%0.334
  • X: (15 − 7)/20 = 0.40. Y: (12 − 7)/10 = 0.50. Z: (18 − 7)/28 = 0.393. Index: (12 − 7)/15 = 0.333.
  • Z has the highest return but ranks 3rd: its extra return came with much more risk.
  • Y and the index earn the same 12%, but Y took two-thirds the risk → Sharpe 0.50 vs 0.33.
  • Our Ganga/Kaveri 60 : 40 portfolio (§2, ρ = 0.3): (13.6 − 7)/21.78 = 0.30.

Decision: Y gives the most return per unit of risk. Combine it with G-Secs or borrowing to reach any risk level you want (next).

Capital Market Line (CML)

  • Add a risk-free asset (T-bill, G-Sec, liquid fund). Mixing it with any risky portfolio gives a straight line in (σ, E(R)) space.
  • The steepest such line touches the efficient frontier at the market portfolio M (the tangency portfolio). That line is the CML; its slope is M's Sharpe ratio.
  • Separation idea: everyone holds the same risky portfolio M; cautious investors add G-Secs, aggressive ones borrow to buy more M.
Capital Market LineE(Rp) = Rf + [(E(Rm) − Rf) / σm] × σp
Worked example — mixing an index fund with G-Secs

Rf = 7%, market (Nifty 50 proxy) E(Rm) = 12%, σm = 15% (illustrative). Slope = (12 − 7)/15 = 0.3333.

Common mistakes
  • Dividing raw return by σ — always subtract Rf first.
  • Comparing a monthly Sharpe with an annual one. Annualise: annual return ≈ monthly × 12, annual σ ≈ monthly σ × √12.
  • Plotting a single stock on the CML. Single stocks belong on the SML.
Recap Sharpe = excess return ÷ σ; pick the highest. CML = Rf + market Sharpe × σp (efficient portfolios); SML uses β (any asset).

5 Valuing bonds (debt)

Explain like I'm 5

A bond is an IOU: you lend a company ₹1,000, it pays you fixed "rent" (coupon) every year and returns the ₹1,000 at the end. The bond's fair price is all those promised rupees, converted into today's rupees.

Bond and share basics — face value, coupon, redemption, YTM, types of bonds and shares — are in Session 10. One-line recap: a bond pays a fixed coupon (coupon rate × face value F) each period and repays F at maturity; the YTM is the return the market currently demands on it. This page is the valuation maths.

Worked example 2 — the same bond with semi-annual coupons

Rule: halve the coupon, halve the YTM, double the periods. C = ₹35, r = 5% per half-year, n = 10.

  • Price = 270.26 + 613.91 = ₹884.17

Interpretation: ₹2.11 less than the annual version — the 5% half-yearly rate compounds to 10.25% a year, slightly more than 10%. Use this method for G-Secs and most Indian bonds.

Zero-coupon bonds and T-bills

Worked example 4 — zero-coupon bond and a 91-day T-bill
  • 3-year zero, F = ₹1,000, YTM 9%: P = 1,000/1.093 = 1,000/1.295029 = ₹772.18. Your whole return is the ₹227.82 gain at maturity.
  • 91-day T-bill (face ₹100) bought at ₹98.25: yield = (100 − 98.25)/98.25 × 365/91 × 100 = 1.75/98.25 × 4.0110 × 100 = 7.14% a year.
Common mistakes
  • Semi-annual: halving the coupon but keeping r = 10% and n = 5 (or vice versa). All three must change together.
  • Discounting at the coupon rate. Discount at the YTM (market rate); the coupon only sets the cash flows.
  • Forgetting the face value in the final year.
  • Mixing quotes: G-Sec and Excel prices are per ₹100 face (88.63), corporate examples often per ₹1,000 (886.28).
Recap Bond price = PV of coupons + PV of face, discounted at YTM. Semi-annual: C/2, r/2, 2n. Coupon vs YTM tells you premium / par / discount.

6 Bond yields, price–yield & duration

Explain like I'm 5

Your old FD pays 7%. Banks now offer 9% on new FDs. Nobody would buy your 7% FD at full price — they'd want a discount. Rates up → old bonds' prices down.

Current yieldCY = Annual coupon / Market price
YTM (approximation)YTM ≈ [C + (F − P)/n] / [(F + P)/2]
  • Current yield ignores the gain/loss from price moving to par at maturity.
  • YTM is the bond's IRR: the r that solves price = PV of coupons + PV of face. It assumes you hold to maturity and reinvest coupons at the YTM. Same formula as the cost of debt in Session 9.
Worked example 1 — YTM three ways

Bond: face ₹1,000, coupon 9% annual (C = ₹90), 6 years to maturity, market price ₹950.

(a) Approximation

  • Numerator = 90 + (1,000 − 950)/6 = 90 + 8.33 = 98.33 (coupon + yearly share of the ₹50 discount)
  • Denominator = (1,000 + 950)/2 = 975 (average amount invested)
  • YTM ≈ 98.33/975 = 10.09%

(b) Trial and error + interpolation

  • At 10%: price = 90 × PVIFA(10%, 6) + 1,000/1.106 = ₹956.45 → above ₹950, so the yield is higher than 10%.
  • At 11%: price = 90 × 4.230538 + 1,000 × 0.534641 = 380.75 + 534.64 = ₹915.39 → below ₹950.
  • YTM = 10% + (956.45 − 950)/(956.45 − 915.39) × 1% = 10% + 6.45/41.06 × 1% = 10.16%

(c) Exact (Excel): =RATE(6, 90, -950, 1000) → 10.15%.

Interpretation: buying at ₹950 and holding 6 years earns ≈ 10.15% a year; current yield is only 90/950 = 9.47% because it ignores the ₹50 gain at maturity.

  • 7% → 8%: 5-yr falls 3.99%, 10-yr falls 6.71%, 10-yr zero falls 8.88% → longer maturity and lower coupon = more sensitive.
  • Asymmetry (convexity): 5-yr bond, 7% → 4% gains ₹133.55, but 7% → 10% loses only ₹113.72. Prices rise more than they fall for the same yield change.
  • Indian link: when RBI cuts the repo rate and yields fall, existing bond prices (and gilt mutual-fund NAVs) rise.

7 Valuing shares (equity): DDM & P/E

Explain like I'm 5

A mango tree is worth all the mangoes it will ever give you, counted in today's money. A share is worth all the dividends it will ever pay you, counted the same way.

  • Intrinsic value = PV of expected future cash flows to the shareholder. Market price = what it trades at on NSE/BSE today.
  • Dividend discount model (DDM): P0 = Σ Dt/(1 + Ke)t, discounted at the cost of equity Ke (from CAPM, Session 12).
  • One-year holder: P0 = (D1 + P1)/(1 + Ke). E.g. D1 = ₹5, expected P1 = ₹120, Ke = 12% → P0 = 125/1.12 = ₹111.61. P1 itself depends on later dividends, so the chain leads back to "all dividends".
Zero growthP0 = D / Ke
Constant growth (Gordon)P0 = D1 / (Ke − g)    with   D1 = D0(1 + g),   Ke > g
Two-stageP0 = Σt=1..N Dt/(1 + Ke)t + PN/(1 + Ke)N    with   PN = DN+1/(Ke − g2)
SymbolMeaning
D0Dividend per share just paid
D1Dividend expected next year
KeCost of equity / required return
gConstant dividend growth rate forever
PNValue at the end of the high-growth stage (terminal value)
Worked example 1 — zero growth

A mature utility (fictional) pays ₹12 a share every year with no growth; Ke = 15%. P0 = 12/0.15 = ₹80. Preference shares with a fixed dividend are valued the same way.

Worked example 2 — Gordon growth: Sahyadri Cements (fictional)

D0 = ₹10 · g = 6% · β = 1.2 · Rf = 7% · Rm = 12% · market price ₹130.

  1. Ke (CAPM) = 7% + 1.2 × (12% − 7%) = 7% + 6% = 13%
  2. D1 = 10 × 1.06 = ₹10.60
  3. P0 = 10.60 / (0.13 − 0.06) = 10.60 / 0.07 = ₹151.43

Decision: intrinsic ₹151.43 > market ₹130 → undervalued → buy. Cross-check: at ₹130 the expected return is D1/P0 + g = 10.60/130 + 6% = 8.15% + 6% = 14.15%, above the 13% required.

Estimating g (recap of Sessions 9–10): historical g = (Dn/D0)1/n − 1, or sustainable g = b × r (retention ratio × ROE), e.g. 0.60 × 15% = 9%.

P/E-based valuation

P/E valuationValue per share = EPS1 × P/E   ·   Justified P/E = (D1/E1) / (Ke − g) = payout ratio / (Ke − g)
Worked example 4 — Tapti Textiles (fictional)

Next year's EPS ₹20 · payout 40% · ROE 15% · Ke 13% · peer textile companies trade at 12× earnings · market price ₹230.

  • g = b × r = 0.60 × 15% = 9%
  • Justified P/E = 0.40/(0.13 − 0.09) = 10× → value = 20 × 10 = ₹200 (same as Gordon: D1 = 0.40 × 20 = ₹8; 8/0.04 = ₹200 ✓)
  • Relative (peer) value = 20 × 12 = ₹240

Interpretation: at ₹230 the stock looks cheap vs peers but expensive vs its own fundamentals. Either the peers are overpriced or the market expects more than 9% growth. Peer P/E is quick; justified P/E tells you why.

Intrinsic value (IV) vs market price (MP)VerdictAction
IV > MPUndervaluedBuy / hold
IV ≈ MPFairly pricedHold
IV < MPOvervaluedSell / avoid

DDM suits steady dividend payers (FMCG, utilities, PSU banks). For companies that pay little or no dividend, use FCFE/FCFF DCF or multiples (Session 10).

Common mistakes
  • Using D0 in the Gordon numerator. It needs D1 = D0(1 + g).
  • Discounting PN by N + 1 years. PN is already a year-N value.
  • Applying Gordon when g ≥ Ke — the answer is negative or infinite; use a two-stage model.
  • Mixing % and decimals: 10.60/(13 − 6) = 1.51 is wrong; use 0.13 − 0.06.
Recap Share value = PV of dividends at Ke: D/Ke (no growth), D1/(Ke − g) (Gordon), or explicit dividends + Gordon terminal value (two-stage). Compare with market price to buy or sell.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Portfolio theory says judge an asset by its effect on the whole portfolio: returns average out, but risk falls whenever correlation is below +1, which leaves only systematic risk. The minimum-variance mix and the efficient frontier show the best risk–return trade-offs; adding a risk-free G-Sec turns the choice into a straight line (CML) whose slope is the Sharpe ratio. Valuing securities uses one rule for both: price = PV of promised or expected cash flows at the required return. For bonds that's coupons plus face value at the YTM; for shares it's dividends at Ke (Gordon, two-stage) or earnings × a P/E. If intrinsic value is above the market price, buy.

Practice & self-check

Stock A: E(R) 14%, σ 25%. Stock B: E(R) 8%, σ 15%. ρ = 0.5. Find E(Rp) and σp for a 50 : 50 portfolio.

E(Rp) = 0.5 × 14 + 0.5 × 8 = 11%. σp2 = 0.25 × 625 + 0.25 × 225 + 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 25 × 15 = 156.25 + 56.25 + 93.75 = 306.25 → σp = 17.5% (below the 20% weighted average because ρ < 1).

Same two stocks. What weight in A gives the minimum-variance portfolio?

Cov = 0.5 × 25 × 15 = 187.5. wA* = (225 − 187.5)/(625 + 225 − 375) = 37.5/475 = 0.0789 → 7.89% in A, 92.11% in B. E(Rp) = 8.47%, σp = 14.90% (less than B's 15%).

Fund P returned 14% with σ 18%; Fund Q returned 11% with σ 9%. Rf = 7%. Which is better on a risk-adjusted basis?

Sharpe P = (14 − 7)/18 = 0.39; Sharpe Q = (11 − 7)/9 = 0.44 → Q. Mix Q with borrowing if you want P's level of return.

A ₹1,000 bond pays 8% coupons semi-annually, matures in 3 years, YTM 6%. Price?

C = ₹40, r = 3%, n = 6. PV coupons = 40 × PVIFA(3%, 6) = 40 × 5.417191 = 216.69. PV face = 1,000/1.036 = 1,000/1.194052 = 837.48. Price = ₹1,054.17 — a premium, because coupon 8% > YTM 6%.

A 10% annual-coupon, 5-year, ₹1,000 bond trades at ₹1,050. Approximate YTM?

[100 + (1,000 − 1,050)/5] / [(1,000 + 1,050)/2] = (100 − 10)/1,025 = 8.78%. Exact (=RATE(5,100,-1050,1000)) = 8.72%. YTM < coupon because the bond trades at a premium.

D0 = ₹4, g = 8%, β = 0.9, Rf = 7%, Rm = 12%. Market price ₹140. Value the share and decide.

Ke = 7% + 0.9 × 5% = 11.5%. D1 = 4 × 1.08 = ₹4.32. P0 = 4.32/(0.115 − 0.08) = 4.32/0.035 = ₹123.43 < ₹140 → overvalued → sell / avoid.

Yields rise by 1%. Which falls more: a 5-year 7% coupon bond or a 10-year zero-coupon bond? Why?

The 10-year zero. Its duration equals its 10-year maturity (all cash at the end), versus about 4.4 years for the 5-year coupon bond (at a 7% yield). Higher duration → bigger price change (7% → 8%: −8.88% vs −3.99%).

An investor holds 50 large Nifty stocks and says her portfolio is "risk-free". Is she right?

No. Diversification removes unsystematic (firm-specific) risk only. Systematic risk — rates, inflation, recessions — remains and is measured by beta. A Nifty crash still hits her portfolio; only hedging (futures, puts) or holding risk-free assets reduces it.
