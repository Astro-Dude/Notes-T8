1 Financial markets in brief

Explain like I'm 5

A sabzi mandi brings farmers and buyers together and the price comes out of their haggling. A stock exchange is a mandi for shares and bonds — savers on one side, companies and governments on the other.

  • Financial market = a place or system where financial instruments (shares, bonds, bills) are issued and traded, moving savers' money to firms and governments.
  • Functions: price discovery · liquidity (you can sell when you want) · mobilising savings · sharing risk · lowering transaction and information costs.

2 Efficient Market Hypothesis

Explain like I'm 5

If the whole hostel knows the canteen gives free samosas at 4 pm, the queue forms at 3:59. News everyone already has can't get you ahead of the queue.

  • Efficient market (Eugene Fama, 1970): security prices fully reflect all available information. New information is absorbed quickly and without bias, so price ≈ best estimate of intrinsic value.
  • Consequence: no investor can consistently earn abnormal returns (returns above what the risk justifies), after costs, using that information.
  • Why it should hold: many profit-seeking analysts compete; information is cheap and fast (exchange filings, news); prices react the moment news arrives.
  • Efficient does not mean prices never change, are always "right", or that nobody ever beats the market — only that nobody does it consistently except by luck or extra risk.

Random walk

Random walk with driftPt = Pt−1 + μ + εt
  • μ = normal expected price rise (the required return); εt = surprise from new information, unpredictable and independent of past ε.
  • News is random by definition (if it were predictable, it wouldn't be news), so price changes are random. Yesterday's move tells you nothing about today's.
  • A random walk is a sign of an efficient market, not an irrational one.

The three forms

FormPrices reflectUseless for beating the marketHow it's testedIllustration
WeakAll past market data — prices, volumesTechnical analysis (charts, moving averages, patterns)Serial-correlation and runs tests; trading-rule (filter) testsA "buy after three green days" rule on Nifty 50 stocks should not beat buy-and-hold after brokerage and taxes.
Semi-strongAll public information — past prices + results, announcements, news, economic dataFundamental analysis of public informationEvent studies: abnormal returns around results, buybacks, bonus issues, RBI rate decisionsA stock moves within minutes of results being filed on NSE/BSE; reading the results an hour later earns nothing extra.
StrongAll information, public and private (insider)Even inside informationReturns of insiders and fund managersRejected in practice — insiders can profit, which is why SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 ban trading on unpublished price-sensitive information (UPSI).

The forms are nested: strong ⊃ semi-strong ⊃ weak. If the market is semi-strong efficient, it is also weak-form efficient.

What EMH implies

ForImplication
InvestorsHard to beat the index after costs → low-cost diversified investing (Nifty 50 index funds/ETFs) is a sensible default; pick risk level, not "winners".
Fund managersOutperformance must be judged after adjusting for risk (beta, §6) and costs; one good year proves little.
CompaniesCosmetic accounting changes (e.g. switching depreciation method with no cash effect) shouldn't move value; market prices are useful signals for managers.
RegulatorsFast, full disclosure and insider-trading enforcement push markets towards efficiency.
Common mistakes
  • "Efficient means prices are always correct" — no; it means errors are unpredictable, not absent.
  • "Someone beat the market, so EMH is false" — with lakhs of investors some will win by luck; the test is consistent, risk-adjusted outperformance.
  • Mixing up the forms: technical analysis ↔ weak; public fundamental analysis ↔ semi-strong; insider information ↔ strong.
Recap Prices reflect information: past prices (weak), all public info (semi-strong), all info incl. private (strong). Price changes follow a random walk. Test with abnormal returns: AR = R − E(R).

3 Anomalies & behavioural finance

Explain like I'm 5

At a mela, everyone crowds the stall with the longest queue, assuming it must be best — while a quieter stall sells the same jalebis cheaper. People aren't always calculators.

An anomaly is a return pattern that EMH (plus a risk model like CAPM) can't explain.

AnomalyWhat's observedChallenges
MomentumStocks that rose most over the past 3–12 months tend to keep outperforming for a whileWeak form
Size (small-cap) effectSmall firms have historically earned higher returns than their beta predictsSemi-strong / CAPM
Value effectLow P/E and low price-to-book stocks have outperformed "glamour" stocksSemi-strong / CAPM
January effectSmall-caps do well in January (US tax-loss selling in December); evidence elsewhere — including India, whose tax year ends 31 March — is mixedWeak form
Day-of-week effectLower average returns on Mondays in some markets and periodsWeak form
Post-earnings announcement driftPrices keep drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeksSemi-strong
IPO pricingLarge listing-day gains, but weak long-run returns for many IPOsSemi-strong (primary market)
Bubbles & crashesPrices far from fundamentals, e.g. the 2000 dot-com bubble, India's 1992 bull run (Harshad Mehta scam)All forms

Why anomalies can persist: trading costs and short-selling limits stop arbitrageurs fixing them; some vanish once published (data mining); and the joint-hypothesis problem — every EMH test is also a test of the risk model, so "abnormal" may just mean "risk not captured by beta".

Behavioural finance — the biases behind anomalies

BiasMeaningIndian-style example
OverconfidenceOver-rating your own skill → trading too muchRetail F&O trading: SEBI's own studies (2023, 2024) found roughly 9 in 10 individual F&O traders made net losses.
HerdingCopying the crowdPiling into a hot IPO or small-cap theme because friends on WhatsApp are

Key takeaway

Working verdict: large, liquid stocks (Nifty 50) are close to semi-strong efficient; small, illiquid stocks less so. Anomalies exist but are hard to exploit after costs — which is why EMH remains the starting assumption.

Recap Momentum, size, value, calendar effects and drift challenge EMH; behavioural biases (overconfidence, herding, loss aversion, anchoring) explain them; limits to arbitrage let them survive.

4 Risk & return

Explain like I'm 5

A bank FD at 7% pays exactly 7%. A share that "averages 12%" might give +30% one year and −15% the next. Risk is how widely the results swing around the average.

Holding-period returnR = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0
Worked example — holding-period return

Bought at ₹500, received ₹10 dividend, sold at ₹560. R = (560 − 500 + 10)/500 = 70/500 = 14% = 2% dividend yield + 12% capital gain.

5 Systematic vs unsystematic risk

Explain like I'm 5

A failed monsoon hurts every farmer (systematic). One farmer's tractor breaking down hurts only him (unsystematic). Owning 30 different farms protects you from tractors, not from the monsoon.

Total riskσi2 = βi2σm2 + σε2   (total = systematic + unsystematic)
Systematic riskUnsystematic risk
Also calledMarket, non-diversifiableSpecific, idiosyncratic, diversifiable
SourcesRBI repo rate changes, inflation, GDP slowdown, elections, oil prices, war, pandemicStrike, CEO exit, plant fire, product recall, losing a key contract, lawsuit
AffectsAlmost all stocksOne company or industry
Removed by diversification?NoYes
Measured byBeta (β)Residual σε
Earns a risk premium?YesNo — you could have diversified it away for free
Worked example — splitting a stock's risk

Stock σ = 30%, β = 1.2, market σm = 18%. Work in %².

  • Total variance = 302 = 900.
  • Systematic = β2σm2 = 1.44 × 324 = 466.56 → 466.56/900 = 51.84% of total.
  • Unsystematic = 900 − 466.56 = 433.44 → σε = √433.44 = 20.82%.

Interpretation: almost half this stock's variance disappears in a diversified portfolio; the market pays a premium only for the 51.84% that stays.

Worked example — how fast diversification works

Equal-weighted portfolio of n stocks, each σ = 30%, every pair correlation ρ = 0.3. σp2 = σ2/n + (1 − 1/n)·ρσ2 = 900/n + (1 − 1/n) × 270.

6 Beta

Explain like I'm 5

Everyone is on the same bus (the market). When it brakes, a calm passenger barely sways (β 0.6); an excitable one lurches 1.5× as far (β 1.5).

  • Beta (β) measures a security's systematic risk: how much its return moves, on average, for a 1% move in the market.
  • β is the slope of the line through (market return, stock return) points.
Betaβi = Cov(Ri, Rm) / Var(Rm) = ρim · σi / σm
βMeaningTypical of (general tendency, not live data)
> 1Aggressive — amplifies market movesMetals, real estate, many PSU banks
= 1Moves with the marketA Nifty 50 index fund
0 to 1Defensive — moves less than the marketFMCG, pharma, utilities
= 0No link to the marketTreasury bill
< 0Moves opposite to the marketGold, at times

  • Rows 10–11 — both .S. The (n − 1)s cancel in the ratio; mixing .S with .P doesn't (0.00475/0.00304 = 1.56 ✗).
  • Row 13 — check: β = ρσi/σm = 0.9113 × 8.456/6.164 = 1.25.
  • Row 14 — R² = ρ² = 0.83: 83% of this stock's variance is systematic (market-driven); 17% is diversifiable.
  • Row 15 — α = 7 − 1.25 × 5 = 0.75% per period: the return not explained by the market.

Beta in practice

  • Use 2–5 years of weekly or monthly returns (not prices) against a broad index (Nifty 50, Nifty 500 or Sensex). Return = (Pt − Pt−1 + D)/Pt−1.
  • Betas drift towards 1 over time. Adjusted (Blume) beta = 0.67 × raw β + 0.33 → 0.67 × 1.25 + 0.33 = 1.17.
  • Portfolio beta = weighted average of betas: βp = Σ wiβi.
Worked example — portfolio beta

40% in Chaiwala (β 1.25), 35% in an FMCG stock (β 0.8), 25% in a metals stock (β 1.6).

βp = 0.40 × 1.25 + 0.35 × 0.8 + 0.25 × 1.6 = 0.50 + 0.28 + 0.40 = 1.18 → slightly more aggressive than the market.

Common mistakes
  • Running SLOPE on prices instead of returns, or with the arguments swapped (gives Cov/Var(Ri) — a different number).
  • Mixing daily stock returns with monthly index returns, or misaligned dates.
  • Thinking high β means high total risk — a stock can be very volatile (high σ) with low β if its swings are firm-specific.
Recap β = Cov(Ri, Rm)/Var(Rm) = SLOPE(stock, market). β > 1 aggressive, < 1 defensive. Portfolio β = weighted average.

Explain like I'm 5

A government bond pays 7% with no risk. If you hold a share that swings 1.25× the market, you want that 7% plus 1.25 times the extra the market pays for taking risk.

Security Market Line (SML)

  • The SML is CAPM drawn as a line: required return (y-axis) against β (x-axis). Intercept = Rf; slope = MRP.
  • Every correctly priced security sits on the line. Compare a security's forecast return with its CAPM required return:

Worked example — from prices to a verdict

Stock X: price today ₹200, expected dividend ₹6, expected price in one year ₹224, β = 1.4.

  1. Expected return = (224 − 200 + 6)/200 = 30/200 = 15%.
  2. Required return = 7% + 1.4 × 5% = 14%.
  3. 15% > 14% → above the SML → undervalued.
  4. Fair price today = (224 + 6)/1.14 = ₹201.75 (rounded to 2 dp) — the price at which X would earn exactly 14%.

Assumptions & limitations

Practical estimation issues: which Rf (91-day T-bill vs 10-year G-Sec — match it to the cash-flow horizon); the MRP (historical vs implied estimates differ by several points); β changes with period, frequency and index. Empirically the SML is flatter than CAPM predicts. Still the standard tool for Ke because it's simple and ties return to diversifiable vs non-diversifiable risk.

Common mistakes
  • Multiplying β by the market return instead of the premium: 7 + 1.25 × 12 = 22% ✗ (correct: 13.25%).
  • Treating "market risk premium" and "market return" as the same thing.
  • Using σ instead of β in CAPM — CAPM prices only systematic risk.
  • Calling a stock "undervalued" because its β is low — the verdict comes from forecast vs required return.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

In a reasonably efficient market, prices already reflect public information, so the reliable way to earn more is to take more of the right kind of risk. Total risk (σ) splits into a diversifiable part the market won't pay for and a systematic part measured by β. CAPM prices that systematic risk: required return = Rf + β × market risk premium. Drawn as the SML, it tells you whether a security is cheap (above the line) or dear (below), and it supplies the cost of equity that feeds WACC and every DCF.

Practice & self-check

Cov(Ri, Rm) = 0.0060 and Var(Rm) = 0.0040. Rf = 7%, E(Rm) = 12%. Find β and the required return.

β = 0.0060/0.0040 = 1.5. Required return = 7% + 1.5 × (12% − 7%) = 7% + 7.5% = 14.5%.

A stock returns 20%, 10% or −4% with probabilities 0.25, 0.50, 0.25. Find E(R) and σ.

E(R) = 0.25 × 20 + 0.5 × 10 + 0.25 × (−4) = 5 + 5 − 1 = 9%. Deviations 11, 1, −13 → σ² = 0.25 × 121 + 0.5 × 1 + 0.25 × 169 = 30.25 + 0.5 + 42.25 = 73 → σ = √73 = 8.54%.

Analysts forecast 13% for a stock with β = 0.9. Rf = 7%, E(Rm) = 12%. Under- or over-valued?

Required = 7 + 0.9 × 5 = 11.5%. α = 13 − 11.5 = +1.5% → above the SML → undervalued (buy).

Price ₹400, expected dividend ₹8, expected price next year ₹440, β = 1.3 (Rf 7%, E(Rm) 12%). Verdict and fair price?

Expected return = (440 − 400 + 8)/400 = 48/400 = 12%. Required = 7 + 1.3 × 5 = 13.5%. 12% < 13.5% → below the SML → overvalued. Fair price = (440 + 8)/1.135 = ₹394.71 (vs ₹400 market).

A stock has σ = 24%, β = 0.9; the market's σ = 20%. What share of its variance is systematic, and what is σε?

Total variance = 24² = 576. Systematic = 0.9² × 20² = 0.81 × 400 = 324 → 324/576 = 56.25%. Unsystematic = 576 − 324 = 252 → σε = √252 = 15.87%.

You hold ₹6,00,000 in a stock with β 1.4 and ₹4,00,000 in one with β 0.9. Portfolio β and required return (Rf 7%, E(Rm) 12%)?

Weights 0.6 and 0.4. βp = 0.6 × 1.4 + 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.84 + 0.36 = 1.20. Required = 7 + 1.2 × 5 = 13%.

(a) A moving-average trading rule earns consistent abnormal returns after costs. (b) A company director buys shares a week before an unannounced merger and profits. Which form of EMH does each contradict?

(a) Weak form — past prices shouldn't predict returns (and so semi-strong and strong fail too). (b) Strong form — private information earned abnormal returns; it is also illegal insider trading on UPSI under SEBI's 2015 regulations. Semi-strong can still hold.

Why does CAPM reward β but not a stock's total standard deviation?

Total σ includes unsystematic risk, which any investor can remove for free by diversifying. In equilibrium nobody is paid for bearing avoidable risk, so only systematic risk — measured by β — earns a premium.
