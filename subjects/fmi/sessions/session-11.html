1 Valuation — what the model answers

About this session

The class deck for this session is "Valuation & Forecasting – 1" (FCF, FCFF, FCFE). The syllabus sheet's Session 10 topics — free cash flows, DCF valuation (FCFF & FCFE) and multiples (P/E, enterprise value) — are covered on this page, and the Excel modeling is built on top of them.

Explain like I'm 5

Buying a chai stall? You don't pay for the kettle — you pay for all the cash the stall will hand you in future, in today's rupees. A financial model is the notebook where you work that out.

  • Valuation = estimating the economic value of an asset, business or company.
  • Value of an asset = present value of the future economic benefits investors expect to receive from it.
  • Valuation is based on: future cash flows · earnings · growth prospects · risk · capital requirements (debt or equity) · market valuations of comparable companies.

Two approaches

  • Intrinsic valuation — what are the company's future cash flows worth today? → DCF on FCFF or FCFE (§3–§5).
  • Relative valuation — how much are similar companies being valued at by the market? → multiples (§6). Side-by-side comparison at the end of §6.
  • Uses: investment decisions · mergers & acquisitions (M&A) · IPO pricing · sale or purchase of a business · strategic decisions.
  • Market price ≠ intrinsic value. Market price = the price the asset is trading at. Intrinsic value = an estimate based on the asset's inherent strength, i.e. its ability to generate future cash flows. Intrinsic > price → looks undervalued.

Six factors that drive the value of a business (slide 5) — and where each sits in the model

FactorQuestionModel line it controls
Cash flowsHow much cash can the business generate?EBIT margin, tax → NOPAT
GrowthHow quickly can cash flows grow?Revenue growth % row
RiskHow uncertain are future cash flows?Discount rate (WACC / Ke)
Investment requirementsHow much must the company reinvest?CapEx, ΔNWC
Capital structureHow much debt and equity does it use?WACC weights; FCFE vs FCFF; net debt in the bridge
Terminal valueWhat is the business worth beyond the forecast period?TV = FCFFn+1/(WACC − g)
Recap Value = PV of future benefits. Intrinsic (DCF) vs relative (multiples). Price is what you pay; intrinsic value is what the cash flows say it's worth.

2 Anatomy of an Excel model

Explain like I'm 5

Like a recipe card: ingredients (inputs) at the top, steps (calculations) in the middle, the dish (value per share) at the bottom. Change one ingredient and the dish updates by itself.

Three blocks, one direction

 (typed once)               (formulas only)    (formulas only)
  • Every number is typed once, in the inputs block. Everything else is a formula pointing at it.
  • Time runs left → right (FY26A, FY27E …); A = actual, E = estimate. Financial year ends 31 March.
  • One formula per row, copied across — so every year is calculated the same way.
  • Units in the label: "₹ crore", "%", "crore shares". Never mix crore and lakh on one sheet.

Colour conventions

  • Blue font = hard-coded input you may change (18% EBIT margin) · black = formula on the same sheet (=C17*$B$3) · green = link to another sheet (=DCF!C22) · optional yellow fill = key output or check cell.

3 Free cash flow: profit → cash

Explain like I'm 5

A kirana store "earns" ₹1,000 but ₹100 is stuck in unsold stock, ₹50 is on customers' credit and it buys a new fridge. The cash it can actually take home is much less than ₹1,000.

  • Accounting profit measures performance; free cash flow focuses on cash available after necessary investment.
  • Free Cash Flow (FCF) = cash generated by a business after making the investments required to maintain and grow the business.
Free cash flow (slide 6)FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure

Class example — ABC Manufacturing Ltd., FY2026 (slides 7–14)

Working-capital sign rule

ChangeEffect on cashIn the formula
Current asset ↑ (inventory, receivables)Cash usedSubtract
Current asset ↓Cash releasedAdd
Current liability ↑ (payables)Cash keptAdd
Current liability ↓Cash paid outSubtract

Combined: ΔNWC = Δinventory + Δreceivables − Δpayables. For ABC: 100 + 50 − 40 = ₹110 crore of extra cash locked in working capital, so OCF = 1,000 + 200 − 110 = 1,090.

Worked example — Chaiwala Pvt. Ltd. (decreases as well as increases)

Given (FY2026): net profit ₹80,00,000 · depreciation ₹15,00,000 · inventory ↑ ₹12,00,000 · receivables ↓ ₹5,00,000 · payables ↓ ₹8,00,000 · new kiosks (capex) ₹30,00,000.

LineRule₹
Net profitstart80,00,000
Add: depreciationnon-cash15,00,000
Less: inventory ↑asset ↑ → subtract(12,00,000)
Add: receivables ↓asset ↓ → add5,00,000
Less: payables ↓liability ↓ → subtract(8,00,000)
Operating cash flow80,00,000
Less: capexcash paid(30,00,000)
Free cash flow50,00,000

OCF equals profit here only because working capital absorbed ₹15 lakh (12 − 5 + 8), exactly cancelling the ₹15 lakh depreciation add-back. FCF = ₹50 lakh — the cash the owners could actually take out.

Common mistakes
  • Adding an increase in receivables ("receivables are an asset, assets are good") — it is cash you haven't collected, so subtract.
  • Subtracting depreciation again — it's already deducted in profit; you add it back.
  • Forgetting capex because "it's not in the P&L" — that is exactly why you must subtract it here.
Recap OCF = net profit + depreciation − increase in current assets + increase in current liabilities. FCF = OCF − capex. ABC: ₹1,000 crore profit → ₹1,090 crore OCF → ₹790 crore FCF.

4 FCFF & FCFE

Explain like I'm 5

A phone shop is funded by the owner and a bank loan. FCFF is the whole cash pie before anyone is paid. FCFE is the owner's slice after the bank takes its EMI (interest) and after any fresh loan is added in.

Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)

  • FCFF = cash generated by operations that is available to all providers of capital — equity shareholders and debt holders.
  • It is the cash flow before payments to debt providers and equity shareholders.
FCFF (slide 15)FCFF = EBIT(1 − T) + Depreciation − CapEx − ΔNWC
SymbolMeaning
EBITEarnings before interest and tax
TCorporate tax rate (decimal: 25% → 0.25)
EBIT(1 − T)= NOPAT, net operating profit after tax — tax as if the firm had no debt
DepreciationNon-cash expense (add back; often written D&A)
CapExCapital expenditure
ΔNWCChange in operating net working capital (increase = cash used)

An alternative using CFO is possible (FCFF ≈ CFO − CapEx), but the EBIT-based formula is particularly useful for valuation because it removes the effect of financing decisions — interest never enters it.

Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)

  • FCFE = cash available specifically to equity shareholders after meeting the cash-flow consequences of debt financing (interest, repayments, new borrowing).
FCFE — net income route (slide 16)FCFE = NI + Depreciation − CapEx − ΔNWC + Net Borrowing
Net borrowingNet Borrowing = New Debt Raised − Debt Repaid
FCFE — from FCFFFCFE = FCFF − Interest(1 − T) + Net Borrowing

The second form shows the relationship: start with the firm's pie, pay lenders their after-tax interest (interest is tax-deductible, so it costs the firm only Interest × (1 − T)), then add any net new borrowing (subtract if net repayment).

Worked example — Dosa Express Ltd., FY2026 (₹ crore)

Key takeaway

Negative FCFF ≠ bad business. It means the company is reinvesting more than it earns; the DCF value depends on those investments producing positive FCFF later.

Worked example — impact of capital structure

Two identical businesses, both with EBIT ₹400 crore, tax 25%, D&A 60, capex 150, ΔNWC 30. Firm U has no debt. Firm L has ₹500 crore debt at 8% (interest 40) and no net borrowing this year.

₹ croreFirm UFirm L
FCFF = 400 × 0.75 + 60 − 150 − 30180180
Net income400 × 0.75 = 300(400 − 40) × 0.75 = 270
FCFE180180 − 40 × 0.75 = 150

Debt changes who gets the cash (and the shareholders' risk, so Ke rises), not how much the operations produce. FCFF is the same; FCFE falls by the after-tax interest.

Common mistakes
  • Subtracting interest inside FCFF, or starting FCFF from net profit — use EBIT(1 − T).
  • Forgetting net borrowing in FCFE, or adding a repayment as if it were new debt (repayment makes net borrowing negative).
  • Subtracting Interest(1 − T) when you started from CFO (full interest) — match the interest treatment to the route.
  • Discounting FCFE at WACC or FCFF at Ke — the cash flow and the rate must belong to the same investors.
Recap FCFF = EBIT(1 − T) + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC (all investors, at WACC). FCFE = NI + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC + net borrowing = FCFF − Interest(1 − T) + net borrowing (shareholders, at Ke).

FCFE route

To value equity directly, build an FCFE row (FCFF − Interest(1 − T) + net borrowing), discount at Ke (e.g. from CAPM, Session 12) and use a terminal value of FCFEn+1/(Ke − g). No debt/cash bridge — the result is already equity value. With consistent assumptions both routes land close; big gaps usually mean the WACC weights and the debt plan disagree.

Common mistakes
  • =NPV(B8,B25:G25) including year 0 — NPV() discounts its first value by one full period. Year-0 amounts go outside it.
  • TV = FCFF5/(WACC − g) — forgets the (1 + g); or discounting TV with the year-6 factor.
  • g ≥ WACC → negative or infinite TV. Keep g at or below long-run nominal GDP growth.
  • Stopping at EV and calling it the share value — subtract debt, add cash, divide by shares.
  • Typing =C17*0.18 instead of =C17*$B$3 — the input then can't be changed from one place.
Recap Revenue × margins → NOPAT → + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC = FCFF → discount at WACC → + PV of TV = EV → − debt + cash = equity → ÷ shares. Kirana Mart: EV ₹2,439.4 crore, ₹213.94 per share.

6 Relative valuation — multiples

Explain like I'm 5

To price a 2BHK flat you don't forecast 30 years of rent — you check the ₹ per sq ft that similar flats in the same society sold for. A multiple is "₹ per sq ft" for companies.

Equity value vs enterprise value

Market capitalisationMarket cap = Share price × Shares outstanding
Enterprise value (EV bridge)EV = Market cap + Debt (+ preference capital + minority interest) − Cash & equivalents   ⇔   Equity value = EV − Debt + Cash
  • Enterprise value = value of the whole business (operations) to all capital providers — what an acquirer pays: buy the shares, take on the debt, keep the cash.
  • Match rule: equity value ↔ metrics after interest (PAT, EPS, book equity); EV ↔ metrics before interest (EBITDA, EBIT, sales).
  • Kirana Mart (§5) at a market price of ₹180: market cap = 180 × 10 crore = ₹1,800 crore → EV = 1,800 + 400 − 100 = ₹2,100 crore → EV/EBITDA = 2,100/220 = 9.5×.

The four multiples

MultipleFormulaGivesUse whenAvoid when
P/EPrice / EPS = Market cap / PATEquity valueStable, profitable firms with similar leverageLosses (negative P/E is meaningless); one-off gains; very different debt levels
EV/EBITDAEV / EBITDAEV → bridge to equityPeers with different debt, tax or depreciation; capital-intensive sectors (telecom, cement, retail); M&ABanks/NBFCs (debt is their raw material); hides capex differences
EV/SalesEV / RevenueEV → bridge to equityLoss-making or early-stage firms, cyclical troughsPeers with very different margins
P/BPrice / Book value per share = Market cap / Book equityEquity valueBanks, NBFCs, insurers, asset-heavy firmsAsset-light IT/services and brands — book equity understates value
  • Trailing P/E uses the last reported (or last 12 months') EPS; forward P/E uses next year's expected EPS. For a growing firm, forward < trailing.
  • Kirana Mart at the DCF value ₹213.94: trailing EPS ₹11.10 (FY26A) → 19.3×; forward EPS FY27E = (207 − 32) × 0.75 / 10 = ₹13.13 (interest assumed flat at ₹32 crore) → 16.3×. Always compare trailing with trailing, forward with forward.

Common mistakes
  • Applying a P/E to EBITDA or an EV multiple to PAT — numerator and denominator must belong to the same investors.
  • Reporting EV × multiple as the equity value — subtract debt, add cash first.
  • Using the mean when one peer is an outlier — use the median.
  • Mixing a peer's forward P/E with the target's trailing EPS.
  • "Peers" that aren't comparable (different business, size, growth or country).
Recap EV = market cap + debt − cash. P/E, P/B → equity; EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales → EV, then − debt + cash. Peer median × target metric → value per share. EV multiples aren't distorted by leverage; use DCF and multiples together.

Explain like I'm 5

Before you lend a friend money for a food truck, you ask "what if sales are 20% lower? what if rent goes up?". Sensitivity tables ask the same "what if" of your valuation, all at once.

Goal Seek: what is the market price implying?

Worked example — implied EBIT margin

Kirana Mart trades at ₹180. What EBIT margin does that price assume (everything else unchanged)?

  1. Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek.
  2. Set cell: B37 · To value: 180 · By changing cell: B3.
  3. Excel iterates and sets B3 ≈ 16.0%.

Interpretation: the market is pricing in a 16% margin vs our 18%. If you're confident in 18%, the stock looks cheap; if 16% is realistic, it's fairly priced.

Common mistakes
  • Data Table input cells on a different sheet — Excel refuses; keep inputs on the table's sheet (why our inputs live on DCF). Swapping row/column input cells fills the grid with wrong numbers, silently.
  • Hard-coded numbers inside formulas, or a typed number overwriting a formula in one year.
  • Mixed units — revenue in ₹ crore, shares in lakh → per-share value off by 100×. Percentages typed as 18 instead of 18%.
  • Unintended circular references (interest ↔ cash ↔ debt) — break them with opening balances or enable iterative calculation deliberately.
Recap Data Table = output grid for two inputs; Goal Seek = one input that hits a target; CHOOSE = scenario switch. NPV/XNPV, IRR/XIRR, PMT, SUMPRODUCT, MEDIAN, XLOOKUP cover most models. Build checks that shout when the model breaks.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

A company is worth the cash it will generate, not the profit it reports. Convert profit to cash (add back depreciation, adjust working capital, subtract capex), decide whose cash you are valuing (FCFF for all investors at WACC, FCFE for shareholders at Ke), forecast it in a clean inputs → calculations → outputs sheet, discount it, add a terminal value and bridge to equity per share. Value it a second way with peer multiples — P/E and P/B give equity value, EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales give EV (then − debt + cash), using the peer median. Then stress-test: Data Tables, Goal Seek and scenarios show how fragile the answer is.

Practice & self-check

Net profit ₹500 crore, depreciation ₹80 crore, inventory up ₹40 crore, receivables down ₹20 crore, payables up ₹10 crore, capex ₹150 crore. Find OCF and FCF.

OCF = 500 + 80 − 40 + 20 + 10 = ₹570 crore (inventory ↑ subtract; receivables ↓ add; payables ↑ add). FCF = 570 − 150 = ₹420 crore — 84% of profit is free cash.

EBIT ₹600 crore, tax 25%, D&A ₹90 crore, capex ₹200 crore, ΔNWC +₹40 crore. FCFF?

NOPAT = 600 × 0.75 = 450. FCFF = 450 + 90 − 200 − 40 = ₹300 crore.

Same firm: interest ₹60 crore, it repaid ₹50 crore of debt and raised none. FCFE?

Net borrowing = 0 − 50 = −50. FCFE = 300 − 60 × 0.75 + (−50) = 300 − 45 − 50 = ₹205 crore. Repaying debt uses shareholders' cash.

Year-5 FCFF ₹200 crore, terminal growth 4%, WACC 10%. Terminal value and its present value?

TV = 200 × 1.04 / (0.10 − 0.04) = 208 / 0.06 = ₹3,466.67 crore (at end of year 5). PV = 3,466.67 / 1.105 = 3,466.67 / 1.61051 = ₹2,152.53 crore.

Peers' median EV/EBITDA is 10×. Target EBITDA ₹500 crore, debt ₹1,200 crore, cash ₹200 crore, 20 crore shares, market price ₹180. Value per share and decision?

EV = 10 × 500 = ₹5,000 crore. Equity = 5,000 − 1,200 + 200 = ₹4,000 crore → ÷ 20 crore = ₹200 per share. Intrinsic ₹200 > price ₹180 → undervalued by about 11% (200/180 − 1) → buy, if you trust the inputs.

You type =C17*B3 in C18 (B3 = EBIT margin) and copy it to D18:G18. What goes wrong and how do you fix it?

B3 is relative, so D18 becomes =D17*C3, E18 =E17*D3 … pointing at empty or wrong cells. Fix: =C17*$B$3 (press F4) so the margin cell stays locked while the revenue reference moves.

Cash flows: year 0 −₹1,000, years 1–3 +₹400 each, in B2:B5; rate 10% in B1. A student writes =NPV(B1,B2:B5). What does it return and what is the correct formula?

Correct NPV = −1,000 + 400 × 2.4869 = −₹5.26, i.e. =NPV(B1,B3:B5)+B2. The student's formula discounts everything one extra year: −5.26/1.10 = −₹4.78. Small here, but wrong in sign-sensitive cases.

Price ₹300; last year's EPS ₹15; next year's expected EPS ₹20. Find trailing and forward P/E. Peers trade at 18× forward earnings — value per share?

Trailing P/E = 300/15 = 20×; forward P/E = 300/20 = 15× (lower because earnings are growing). Value = 18 × 20 = ₹360 → 20% above ₹300 → undervalued. Compare forward with forward: applying 18× to trailing EPS (₹270) would mix bases.
