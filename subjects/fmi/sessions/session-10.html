1 Debt as finance & what a bond is

Explain like I'm 5

A bond is an IOU. You lend ₹1,000 to an Indian issuer today; it promises fixed interest every year and gives your ₹1,000 back on a fixed date.

Features of debt as a source of finance (class list)

#FeatureMeaning
1Contractual obligationIssuer must pay interest and repay principal whether or not it makes a profit
2Fixed periodic paymentsThe coupon is pre-set — unlike equity dividends, which the board decides
3Priority over equityDebt holders are paid before shareholders, even on liquidation
4No ownership / votingBondholders are creditors, not owners
5Finite maturity (usually)Fixed repayment date; perpetual bonds are the exception
6Tax-deductible interestInterest lowers taxable income — the tax shield (Session 9 §5)

Class illustration: when Reliance Industries issued ₹20,000 crore of secured NCDs (face value ₹1,00,000 each) in Nov 2023, it took on a fixed obligation to pay interest and repay principal by 2033 — without giving investors any ownership.

Meaning of a bond

  • A bond is a debt security issued by a company, bank, PSU or the Government of India to raise long-term funds.
  • The issuer promises a fixed periodic coupon (interest) and repayment of the face value (principal) on a stated maturity / redemption date.
  • A company's bond is usually called an NCD (non-convertible debenture) in India.

2 Bond terms & coupon bonds

Explain like I'm 5

A bond's price is what its future coupons and the final ₹1,000 are worth today, at the return buyers want right now. If buyers want more than the coupon, they pay less than ₹1,000.

Worked example — semi-annual G-Sec (illustrative)

G-Secs pay coupons every six months. Face ₹100, coupon 7.2% a year, 3 years left, required yield 7.8% a year.

  1. Per half-year: coupon = 7.2 ÷ 2 = ₹3.60; rate = 7.8% ÷ 2 = 3.9%; periods = 3 × 2 = 6.
  2. PV of coupons = 3.60 × PVIFA(3.9%, 6) = 3.60 × 5.2592 = ₹18.93.
  3. PV of face = 100 ÷ 1.0396 = 100 ÷ 1.2580 = ₹79.49.
  4. Price = 18.93 + 79.49 = ₹98.42 — a discount, because the 7.2% coupon is below the 7.8% yield.
Excel — bond price
=-PV(9%, 3, 80, 1000)          → 974.69   (rate, nper, coupon, face; PV returns a negative, so flip the sign)
=-PV(8%, 2, 100, 1000)         → 1,035.67
=-PV(7.8%/2, 3*2, 7.2/2, 100)  → 98.42    (semi-annual: halve rate and coupon, double periods)

Common mistakes
  • Discounting at the coupon rate — always discount at the required yield (otherwise every bond comes out at par).
  • Forgetting the face value in the last year, or adding it to every year.
  • Semi-annual bonds: halving the coupon but not the rate, or not doubling the periods.
Recap Price = PV of coupons + PV of face value at the required yield. Coupon < yield → discount; coupon > yield → premium; equal → par. Price ↓ when yields ↑.

3 Zero-coupon & perpetual bonds

Explain like I'm 5

A zero-coupon bond is like buying a ₹1,00,000 voucher today for ₹71,299 that you can cash only after 5 years. A perpetual bond is like a pension that pays forever but never gives your capital back.

Zero-coupon bond

Zero-coupon bondP = F / (1 + r)T
  • Pays no periodic interest; bought at a deep discount; the whole return is the gap between purchase price and the nominal value received at redemption.
  • No coupon rate — hence "zero-coupon".
  • No reinvestment risk — there are no coupons to reinvest at uncertain future rates.
  • Full return at redemption: return = redemption value − purchase price.
Worked examples — zero-coupon (class slides; illustrative)

Required yield r · maturity T7% · 5 years8% · 10 years

Price = F ÷ (1 + r)T≈ ₹71,299≈ ₹4,632
Gain at redemption≈ ₹28,701≈ ₹5,368

Check: (1,00,000 ÷ 71,299)1/5 − 1 = 7.0% a year — the yield locked in. REC suits goal-based saving: pay ₹4,632, get ₹10,000 in 10 years, nothing to reinvest in between.

Worked example — a Treasury Bill is a zero-coupon bond (illustrative)

RBI auctions 91-, 182- and 364-day T-Bills at a discount; they are redeemed at face value. A 364-day T-Bill of face ₹100 is bought at ₹93.20.

  • Gain = 100 − 93.20 = ₹6.80 → return = 6.80 ÷ 93.20 = 7.30% for 364 days.
  • Annualised yield = 7.30% × 365/364 = 7.32%.

Perpetual bond

Perpetual bondP = C / r
  • No maturity date — the issuer pays the coupon forever (subject to a call option); the face value is never redeemed, so it plays no role in the price.
  • In India: Additional Tier-1 (AT1) bonds issued by banks (SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, PNB) under Basel III. They rank just above equity and can be written down under regulatory stress — Yes Bank's AT1 bonds (≈ ₹8,400 crore) were written off in 2020 on RBI's direction.
  • Lesson: a bond's safety depends on where it sits in the capital structure, not on being called a "bond".

Common mistakes
  • Putting the face value into the perpetual formula — it's never repaid.
  • Writing a zero's price as F × (1 + r)T — you divide (discount), not multiply.
  • Treating AT1 bonds as being as safe as fixed deposits.
Recap Zero-coupon: P = F/(1 + r)T, whole return at redemption, no reinvestment risk. Perpetual: P = C/r, no redemption, very rate-sensitive.

4 Yield to maturity (YTM)

Explain like I'm 5

YTM is the bond's true interest rate: the coupon you collect plus the gain (or loss) as the price drifts back to face value, per rupee you actually paid.

  • YTM = total return earned if the bond is bought at its current market price and held to redemption — coupon income + capital gain/loss versus nominal value.
  • Exactly, YTM is the bond's IRR: the r that makes the PV of its cash flows equal the market price (the §2 formula solved for r).
  • It is the market's current cost of borrowing for that issuer — the pre-tax cost of debt in WACC (Session 9 §5).
YTM — approximation for hand calculationYTM ≈ [ C + (F − P)/n ] / [ (F + P)/2 ]
SymbolMeaning
CAnnual coupon (₹)
F, PFace value, current market price
nYears to maturity
(F − P)/nAnnual share of the gain (positive if P < F) or loss (negative if P > F)
(F + P)/2Average amount invested over the bond's life
PriceYTM vs coupon rateWhy
Below face valueYTM > coupon rateInvestor gains on redemption
Above face valueYTM < coupon rateInvestor loses a little on redemption

Coupon 9% but true yield ≈ 10.1% (bought below face); a ₹50 discount lifts the 7% bond's yield to ≈ 8.9%.

Worked example — a premium NCD (fictional)

F = ₹1,000, coupon 9% (C = ₹90), 4 years left, market price ₹1,050.

  1. (F − P)/n = (1,000 − 1,050)/4 = −12.50 — a loss spread over 4 years.
  2. YTM ≈ (90 − 12.50) ÷ [(1,000 + 1,050)/2] = 77.50 ÷ 1,025 = 7.56% (exact 7.51%).
  3. Current yield = C ÷ P = 90 ÷ 1,050 = 8.57% — ignores the ₹50 loss at redemption.

Interpretation: the 9% coupon overstates what a buyer at ₹1,050 earns; the true return is about 7.5%.

Common mistakes
  • Reporting the coupon rate (or current yield) as the YTM.
  • Sign of (F − P): for a premium bond it is negative, so YTM falls below the coupon.
  • Dividing by F or by P instead of the average (F + P)/2.
Recap YTM ≈ [C + (F − P)/n] / [(F + P)/2]; exact via =RATE. Discount bond: YTM > coupon; premium bond: YTM < coupon. YTM = pre-tax cost of debt.

5 Stocks — zero & constant growth

Explain like I'm 5

For a share, dividends play the role of coupons — but they can grow, they never stop, and there is no ₹1,000 to get back at the end.

Types of shares

Zero-growth dividend discount model

Zero growthP0 = D1 / r

Dividend constant forever — a perpetuity. Suits mature, no-growth firms that distribute nearly all their profit.

Constant-growth (Gordon) model

Constant growth (Gordon)P0 = D1 / (r − g),   where D1 = D0(1 + g)
  • Fits stable companies growing at a modest, sustainable rate forever.
  • Only valid while r > g — breaks down for companies in a high-growth phase (use §6).
  • Rearranged, it is Session 9's cost of equity: Ke = D1/P0 + g.

Common mistakes
  • Using D0 in the numerator instead of D1 = D0(1 + g). "Just paid" = D0; "expected next year" = D1.
  • Applying Gordon when g ≥ r (zero or negative denominator).
  • Writing r − g as 14 − 10 = 4 and dividing by 4 instead of 0.04.
Recap Zero growth: P0 = D1/r. Gordon: P0 = D1/(r − g), r > g. Value is very sensitive to g — always ask what growth the market price implies.

6 Two-stage growth & bonds vs stocks

Explain like I'm 5

A young company grows fast for a few years, then settles down like any other. Value the fast years one by one, then value the "settled" company with Gordon and bring that number back to today.

Two-stage growth modelP0 = D1[1 − ((1 + g1)/(1 + r))n] / (r − g1) + Pn/(1 + r)n,   Pn = Dn+1/(r − g2)
SymbolMeaning
g1, nStage 1 — high growth rate for a defined number of years n
g2Stage 2 — sustainable long-run growth forever after (must be < r)
PnTerminal price at the end of year n = Gordon value of Dn+1
First termPV of the high-growth dividends; you can also just discount D1 … Dn one by one

Value = PV of high-growth dividends + PV of the eventual steady-state price. Common for Indian IT/consumer firms expected to out-grow the economy for a few years. (The slide prints the last term's exponent as t; it is n — the year at which Pn is valued.)

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Bonds and shares are both promises of future cash, valued as the present value of that cash. A bond's cash is fixed — coupons plus face value — so its price depends on the yield the market demands: above face value when the coupon beats the yield, below when it doesn't, and YTM gives the true return at today's price. Zero-coupon and perpetual bonds are the two extreme cases. A share's cash is dividends with no redemption, discounted at the cost of equity: constant (D1/r), steadily growing (D1/(r − g)) or fast-then-steady (two-stage) — and the growth assumption drives most of the answer.

Practice & self-check

A ₹1,000 bond pays a 10% annual coupon and matures in 3 years. Required yield is 12%. Price?

P = 100/1.12 + 100/1.122 + 1,100/1.123 = 89.286 + 79.719 + 782.958 = ₹951.96 → discount, since coupon < yield.

A zero-coupon bond redeems at ₹1,000 in 6 years. Required yield 8%. Price, and the investor's gain?

1.086 = 1.5869 → P = 1,000 ÷ 1.5869 = ₹630.17. Gain at redemption = 1,000 − 630.17 = ₹369.83.

A perpetual bond (face ₹100) pays a 12% coupon; investors require 10%. Price? Discount or premium?

C = ₹12 → P = 12 ÷ 0.10 = ₹120 → premium, because the 12% coupon beats the 10% required yield.

₹1,000 face, 8% coupon, 5 years to maturity, market price ₹920. Approximate YTM?

[80 + (1,000 − 920)/5] ÷ [(1,000 + 920)/2] = (80 + 16) ÷ 960 = 10.0% — above the 8% coupon because the bond trades at a discount.

A share just paid ₹5; dividends grow 6% forever; required return 11%. Value? And a share paying a constant ₹12 at r = 15%?

D1 = 5 × 1.06 = 5.30 → P0 = 5.30 ÷ 0.05 = ₹106. Zero growth: 12 ÷ 0.15 = ₹80.

D0 = ₹2; dividends grow 20% for 2 years, then 5% forever; r = 12%. Value per share?

D1 = 2.40, D2 = 2.88, D3 = 2.88 × 1.05 = 3.024 → P2 = 3.024 ÷ 0.07 = 43.20. PV = 2.40/1.12 + 2.88/1.2544 + 43.20/1.2544 = 2.143 + 2.296 + 34.439 = ₹38.88.

RBI raises rates and market yields rise 1 point. What happens to existing bond prices, and which falls most: a 2-year coupon bond, a 10-year zero, or a perpetual?

Prices fall (price and yield move inversely). The further out the cash flows, the bigger the fall: the perpetual falls most (e.g. BoB AT1 ₹94.12 → ₹84.21, −10.5%), then the 10-year zero; the 2-year bond falls least.

A bond has a 7% coupon and trades at ₹950 (face ₹1,000, 3 years). Rank coupon rate, current yield and YTM, and explain why the holder still gets ₹1,000 at maturity.

Discount bond → coupon 7% < current yield 70/950 = 7.37% < YTM ≈ 8.89%. The redemption value is fixed by the bond contract, whatever price an investor paid in the market.
