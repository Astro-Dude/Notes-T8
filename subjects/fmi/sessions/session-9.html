1 Cost of capital & sources of finance

Explain like I'm 5

You open a chai stall with ₹60,000 borrowed from your uncle at 10% and ₹40,000 from a friend who wants 20% (he takes more risk). Your money costs you 0.6 × 10% + 0.4 × 20% = 14% on average. If the stall earns less than 14%, someone is unhappy.

  • Cost of capital = the minimum rate of return the firm must generate to meet the required returns of its investors (lenders + shareholders).
  • It is an opportunity cost: investors could earn this much on other investments with a similar risk profile.
  • Other names: hurdle rate, required return, discount rate.
  • Where you use it: discount rate for NPV and the IRR hurdle (Session 8); discount rate for DCF valuation (Session 11: FCFF at WACC, FCFE at Ke); required return when pricing bonds and shares (Session 10).

Equity vs debt — why equity costs more

FeatureEquity shares (class list)Debt (class list)
ClaimResidual claim — paid last, after debt & preference holders, but owns residual profits/assetsPriority over equity — paid before shareholders, even on liquidation
PaymentDividends not fixed or guaranteed — board's discretion; returns mainly via capital appreciationContractual obligation with fixed periodic payments (coupon), regardless of profit
ControlVoting rights — usually one share = one voteNo ownership / voting — lenders are creditors
LifePerpetual capital — no maturityFinite maturity (perpetual bonds excepted)
Investor's downsideLimited liability — loss capped at amount investedLoss only if the firm defaults
TaxDividends paid from post-tax profit — no tax savingInterest is tax-deductible → tax shield
ResultRiskier for the investor → higher required returnSafer + tax-deductible → cheapest source

Usual ordering: Kd(1 − t) < Kp < Ke. Class illustration: Reliance's ₹20,000 crore secured NCDs (face value ₹1,00,000 each, Nov 2023, repayable by 2033) created a fixed obligation to pay interest and principal without giving investors any ownership.

Ordinary shares vs dual-class shares

Ordinary sharesDual / multi-class shares
One share = one vote (proportional control)Same economic (dividend) rights, different voting rights
Voting power matches economic investmentFounders hold high-vote shares to retain control
Standard for most listed companiesPublic gets low/no-vote shares (Alphabet, Meta)
  • Alphabet (class case): Class A (GOOGL) 1 vote, Class B 10 votes (founders only, never traded), Class C (GOOG) no vote → Page & Brin ≈ 52.7% of votes with < 12% of the economic stake.
  • Meta: Class A 1 vote, Class B 10 votes (≈ 99.7% held by Zuckerberg) → ≈ 61% of votes with ≈ 13–14% economic interest.
  • Lesson: a small economic stake can give majority control — the separation of ownership and control.
Worked example — voting vs economic stake (fictional Zaika Tech Ltd.)

Founders hold 1 crore Class B shares (10 votes each); the public holds 9 crore Class A shares (1 vote each). Both classes get the same dividend per share.

  • Economic stake = 1 ÷ (1 + 9) = 10% of dividends and of the company's value.
  • Votes: founders 1 crore × 10 = 10 crore; public 9 crore × 1 = 9 crore → founders' voting power = 10 ÷ 19 = 52.6%.

Interpretation: founders control every ordinary resolution while owning a tenth of the cash flows.

Recap Cost of capital = minimum return investors require = opportunity cost. Equity is riskiest for investors (residual, no fixed payment) so Ke is highest; debt is cheapest (priority + tax-deductible interest).

2 Risk & beta

Explain like I'm 5

If the monsoon fails, every kirana in town sells less — that's systematic risk. If one shop's fridge breaks, only that shop suffers — unsystematic. Owning ten shops removes the fridge problem, not the monsoon problem.

3 Cost of equity I — CAPM & alpha

Explain like I'm 5

A government bond pays 7% with no risk. To hold a jumpy share instead, you want 7% plus a "fear bonus" — and the jumpier the share (higher beta), the bigger the bonus.

Capital Asset Pricing ModelKe = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)
SymbolMeaningIndian input (illustrative)
KeCost of equity — minimum return equity investors require—
RfRisk-free rate — return on a riskless asset10-year G-Sec yield, e.g. 7%
βBeta — sensitivity of the stock to market movementsFrom §2 or a data provider
RmExpected return on the broad market indexSensex / Nifty 50, e.g. 12–13%
Rm − RfMarket risk premium — extra return for bearing market riske.g. 5–6%
Worked examples — class slides
CompanyRfβRmSubstitutionKe
Infosys7%1.1012%7% + 1.10 × (12% − 7%) = 7% + 5.5%12.50%
Reliance7%0.8313%7% + 0.83 × 6% = 7% + 4.98%11.98%
TCS7%1.1313%7% + 1.13 × 6% = 7% + 6.78%13.78%

Reliance (β < 1) gets a modest risk premium; TCS (β > 1) a higher required return. High beta → higher cost of equity.

Worked example — Sahyadri Appliances Ltd. (fictional; used again in §5 and §6)

Given: Rf = 7% (0.07), β = 1.2, Rm = 13% (0.13).

Ke = 0.07 + 1.2 × (0.13 − 0.07) = 0.07 + 0.072 = 0.142 = 14.2%.

Same inputs, different betas:

Type of stockββ × 6% premiumKe
Defensive FMCG0.63.6%10.6%
Index-like1.06.0%13.0%
Sahyadri1.27.2%14.2%
Aggressive tech1.59.0%16.0%

Interpretation: Sahyadri's shareholders expect at least 14.2% a year; projects financed by them must beat that.

Alpha — skill vs risk

Jensen's alphaα = Ractual − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)]
  • α = 0 → performed exactly as expected for its risk · α > 0 → outperformed · α < 0 → underperformed.
  • Alpha isolates skill from risk-taking.
Worked example — Fund A vs Fund B (class slides)

Benchmark Rm = 10%, Rf = 4% → premium 6%.

LineFund AFund BHow
Beta1.50.8Given
Actual return14%12%Given
CAPM expected13.0%8.8%4% + 1.5 × 6% · 4% + 0.8 × 6%
Alpha+1.0%+3.2%Actual − expected

Fund A has the higher headline return, but Fund B took less risk and delivered more excess return → more manager skill.

Class example 2 — active vs passive: ₹1 lakh each at similar risk; Nifty 50 index fund 20%, active large-cap fund 25% → α ≈ 25% − 20% = +5% (with β ≈ 1 the CAPM expectation is just the market return). That 5% is the manager's stock-selection and timing skill.

Worked example — your own fund check

A mid-cap fund returned 15% with β = 1.2; Rf = 7%, Rm = 12%.

Expected = 7% + 1.2 × 5% = 13% → α = 15% − 13% = +2%. Beat its risk-adjusted target by 2 percentage points.

Common mistakes
  • Multiplying beta by Rm instead of the premium: 7% + 1.2 × 13% = 22.6% ✗ — the right answer is 14.2%.
  • Being given the premium (6%) and subtracting Rf again. Read whether the question gives Rm or Rm − Rf.
  • Ranking funds by raw return instead of alpha.
Recap Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf): risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium. Alpha = actual − CAPM expected; positive alpha = skill.

4 Cost of equity II — Dividend Growth Model

Explain like I'm 5

You buy a share for ₹100. It pays you ₹4 next year and the dividend grows 8% every year. Your return is the 4% you pocket plus the 8% growth = 12%.

  • Also called the Gordon Growth Model: a share is worth the PV of all future dividends growing at a constant rate g forever.
  • Rearranging the price formula gives the return shareholders expect = cost of equity.
Price formP0 = D1 / (Ke − g)
Cost of equity formKe = D1 / P0 + g,   where D1 = D0 × (1 + g)
SymbolMeaning
P0Current market price of the share
D0Most recent (current) dividend per share
D1Expected dividend next year = D0(1 + g)
gConstant expected growth rate of dividends
D1/P0Expected dividend yield

The model breaks down when g ≥ Ke (the denominator becomes zero or negative).

HUL's shareholders require ≈ 17.1%, Infosys's ≈ 14.0% (rounded to 1 dp; exact 17.09% and 14.02%).

Estimating g — two methods

Method 1 — geometric mean of historical dividendsg = (Dn / D0)1/n − 1
  • D0 = earliest dividend, Dn = latest, n = number of years between them.
  • Reflects the actual track record but is distorted by one-off special dividends.
Method 2 — sustainable growthg = b × r,   b = 1 − payout ratio = 1 − DPS/EPS,   r = ROE = Net income / Equity
  • b = retention ratio: share of earnings reinvested. Low b → mature payer (FMCG); high b → growth firm.
  • Growth comes from reinvested profit earning ROE — what the company can realistically repeat.

Extra check (worked here): plug the sustainable g into the DGM. HUL: 53 × 1.016 = ₹53.85 → 53.85 ÷ 2,209 = 2.44% → Ke = 2.44% + 1.6% = 4.0%, below the 7% risk-free rate — impossible, so DGM is unreliable for HUL. Infosys: 45 × 1.097 = ₹49.37 → 3.29% + 9.70% = 13.0%, close to the 14.0% above.

Worked example — Narmada Paints Ltd. (fictional)

Given: EPS ₹30, DPS (D0) ₹12, ROE 18%, price ₹480; dividend 5 years ago ₹7.50.

  1. Payout = 12 ÷ 30 = 40% → b = 60% → g = 0.60 × 0.18 = 10.8%.
  2. D1 = 12 × 1.108 = ₹13.30 → yield = 13.296 ÷ 480 = 2.77%.
  3. Ke = 2.77% + 10.8% = 13.57%.
  4. Cross-check with Method 1: g = (12 ÷ 7.5)1/5 − 1 = 9.86% → D1 = ₹13.18 → Ke = 2.75% + 9.86% = 12.60%.

Interpretation: both methods give 12.6–13.6%, so a cost of equity of about 13% is defensible.

CAPM vs Dividend Growth Model (class table)

BasisCAPMDividend Growth Model
ApplicabilityAll listed companies (dividend or not)Only dividend-paying companies
Risk measurementExplicit — via betaImplicit / ignored
Data requiredRf, β, RmPrice, expected dividend, growth rate
Key assumptionEfficient marketsConstant, perpetual dividend growth
SensitivityBeta & market-premium estimatesHighly sensitive to g
Best suited forGrowth/tech & all-sector firmsStable, mature, dividend-paying firms

They are complementary: compute both and investigate if they disagree widely.

Common mistakes
  • Using D0 in the formula instead of D1 = D0(1 + g).
  • Counting dividends instead of years: FY2021 → FY2025 is n = 4, not 5.
  • Applying DGM when g ≥ Ke, or to a firm that pays no dividend.
  • Using a growth rate inflated by a special dividend (Infosys FY2024) without judgement.
Recap Ke = D1/P0 + g = dividend yield + growth. Estimate g by geometric mean of past dividends or b × ROE; trust DGM only when both agree and g < Ke.

5 Cost of debt & the tax shield

Explain like I'm 5

Your shop pays ₹10 interest. Because interest is deducted before tax, your tax bill falls by ₹2.50 (at 25%). The loan really costs you only ₹7.50 — the taxman pays the rest.

Which rate is the pre-tax cost of debt? (class rules)

If the firm has…Pre-tax cost of debt rD =
Term loanThe current interest rate the lender charges
Issued bonds / NCDsThe bond's yield to maturity (YTM) — not the coupon rate
Loans and bonds togetherA weighted average of each source's cost, using market values
After-tax cost of debtKd after tax = rD × (1 − TC)

YTM — the bond's true yield

  • Face value F (₹100 / ₹1,000 / ₹1,00,000) — repaid at redemption. Coupon C = coupon rate × F.
  • Market price P — may be below (discount), at (par) or above (premium) F.
  • YTM = total return if bought at P and held to redemption (coupon income + gain/loss vs face value). It's the bond's IRR.
YTM approximationYTM ≈ [ C + (F − P)/n ] / [ (F + P)/2 ]

FFace (redemption) value
PCurrent market price (or net issue proceeds for a new issue)
nYears to maturity
(F − P)/nGain (or loss) per year from buying away from face value
(F + P)/2Average amount invested

Worked example — Sahyadri Appliances: cost of its two debts (fictional)

Term loan ₹440 crore at a current rate of 9.5%. NCDs: 40 lakh NCDs, face ₹1,000, coupon 8%, 5 years left, trading at ₹950 (market value 40 lakh × ₹950 = ₹380 crore). Tax 25%.

  1. NCD YTM ≈ [80 + (1,000 − 950)/5] ÷ [(1,000 + 950)/2] = 90 ÷ 975 = 9.23% (exact =RATE(5,80,-950,1000) = 9.30%). Not the 8% coupon.
  2. After tax: term loan 9.5% × 0.75 = 7.125%; NCDs 9.23% × 0.75 = 6.92%.
  3. Weighted pre-tax Kd (market values) = (440 × 9.5% + 380 × 9.23%) ÷ 820 = (41.80 + 35.08) ÷ 820 = 9.375% → after tax 9.375% × 0.75 = 7.03%.

Interpretation: each rupee of Sahyadri's debt costs ≈ 7% after tax, half its 14.2% cost of equity.

Cost of preference shares

Irredeemable preference sharesKp = Dp / P0
  • Dp = fixed preference dividend per share; P0 = market price (or net issue proceeds).
  • No (1 − t): preference dividend is paid out of post-tax profit, so there is no tax shield.
  • Sahyadri: 9% preference shares, face ₹100 → Dp = ₹9; price ₹90 → Kp = 9 ÷ 90 = 10%.
  • Redeemable preference shares: use the YTM-style approximation with Dp in place of C.
Common mistakes
  • Using the coupon rate as the cost of debt — use YTM (current market cost).
  • Forgetting (1 − t) on debt, or applying it to Ke / Kp.
  • Assuming a tax shield for a loss-making company — no taxable profit, no immediate saving.
  • Writing YTM ≈ C ÷ P (that's current yield; it ignores the gain to face value).
Recap Pre-tax cost of debt = current loan rate or bond YTM; after-tax = rD(1 − T). Preference cost = Dp/P0 with no tax adjustment.

6 WACC — weights & full examples

Explain like I'm 5

Your CGPA weights each subject's grade by its credits. WACC weights each money source's cost by how much of the company's money it supplies.

WACC — debt + ordinary equity (class form)WACC = rD(1 − TC) × D/(E + D) + rE × E/(E + D)

Which weights?

  • Market value of equity = share price × shares outstanding. Book value of debt is a common proxy for its market value (the gap is usually small).

Using WACC as the hurdle rate

Worked example — a project funded "by a loan"

Sahyadri can spend ₹50 crore on a new motor line returning ₹13 crore a year for 5 years. The CFO says "it's funded by a term loan, so discount at 7.03%".

  • At WACC 12.5%: PVIFA(12.5%, 5) = 3.5606 → PV = 13 × 3.5606 = ₹46.29 crore → NPV = −₹3.71 crore → reject. IRR = 9.43% < 12.5%.
  • At 7.03% (wrong): NPV = +₹3.26 crore → would wrongly accept.

Interpretation: the loan uses up borrowing capacity that the whole firm (and its shareholders) stand behind. Use the WACC of the project's risk, not the cost of whichever source paid for it.

WACC falls as cheap, tax-shielded debt replaces equity, then rises once lenders and shareholders demand much more for the extra financial risk. The lowest WACC (here ≈ 40% debt) maximises firm value.

Common mistakes
  • Using book weights when market values are available (understates equity for growth firms).
  • Putting the pre-tax cost of debt into WACC, or taxing equity/preference.
  • Using the coupon rate instead of YTM, or the historical loan rate instead of today's rate.
  • Weights that don't sum to 100% (e.g. forgetting preference shares).
  • Mixing % and decimals: 0.75 × 14.2 = 10.65 (%) — keep units consistent.
  • Using one company-wide WACC for a project much riskier than the firm's average.
Recap WACC = Σ (weight × after-tax cost), weights at market value (target structure). Market WACC is usually above book WACC because market equity exceeds book equity. It's the hurdle rate for average-risk projects and the discount rate for FCFF.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

A company's money comes from shareholders, preference holders and lenders, and each wants a return that matches its risk. Equity is riskiest, so its cost comes from CAPM (risk-free rate + beta × market premium) or the Dividend Growth Model (yield + growth). Debt is cheapest: today's loan rate or bond YTM, cut by the tax shield to rD(1 − T). WACC weights these costs by market values and becomes the hurdle rate for projects (Session 8) and the discount rate for valuing the whole firm (Session 11).

Practice & self-check

Rf = 7%, β = 1.4, Rm = 12%. Find Ke.

Ke = 7% + 1.4 × (12% − 7%) = 7% + 7% = 14%.

Cov(Ri, Rm) = 0.0009 and Var(Rm) = 0.0006. Find β and Ke if Rf = 7% and the market risk premium is 6%.

β = 0.0009 ÷ 0.0006 = 1.5. Ke = 7% + 1.5 × 6% = 16% (the premium is given directly — don't subtract Rf again).

A share trades at ₹160, just paid a dividend of ₹8, and dividends grow 6% a year. Cost of equity?

D1 = 8 × 1.06 = ₹8.48. Ke = 8.48 ÷ 160 + 6% = 5.3% + 6% = 11.3%.

EPS ₹20, DPS ₹8, ROE 15%. Sustainable growth rate?

Payout = 8 ÷ 20 = 40% → b = 60% → g = 0.60 × 15% = 9%.

A ₹1,000 bond with a 10% coupon, 4 years to maturity, trades at ₹1,040. Tax 25%. Approximate YTM and after-tax cost of debt?

YTM ≈ [100 + (1,000 − 1,040)/4] ÷ [(1,000 + 1,040)/2] = (100 − 10) ÷ 1,020 = 8.82% (premium bond → below the 10% coupon). After tax = 8.82% × 0.75 = 6.62%.

Equity ₹600 crore at Ke 15%, debt ₹400 crore at 10% pre-tax, tax 25% (market values). WACC?

Weights 60% / 40%. WACC = 0.6 × 15% + 0.4 × 10% × 0.75 = 9% + 3% = 12%.

A fund returned 11% with β = 0.9 when Rf = 6% and the market returned 11%. Did the manager add value?

CAPM expected = 6% + 0.9 × 5% = 10.5% → α = 11% − 10.5% = +0.5%. Yes, slightly — it matched the market with less risk.

Why are market-value weights preferred to book-value weights, and which way does the choice usually move WACC?

Market values show what investors currently expect and what capital costs now (best proxy for the target structure); book values are historical. Market equity usually exceeds book equity, so market weights put more weight on costly equity → market WACC is usually higher (Reliance 12.2% vs 11.1%).
