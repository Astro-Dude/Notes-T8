1 Payoff vs profit & motives

Explain like I'm 5

At a mela game the prize you collect is the payoff; the prize minus the ₹50 ticket is your profit. A ₹40 prize on a ₹50 ticket is a payoff of 40 but a loss of 10.

PayoffProfit (P&L)
MeaningValue of the position at expiryPayoff net of premium paid / received
Long optionmax(ST − K, 0) or max(K − ST, 0); never negativePayoff − premium; can be negative
Short option−payoff; never positivePremium − payoff
DiagramKink sits on the x-axisSame shape shifted down (long) or up (short) by the premium

Convention on this page: profit at expiry, per share, ignoring interest on the premium (standard simplification).

Recipe for any payoff table

  1. List every leg: +S (own stock), −S (short stock), +C / −C (bought / written call), +P / −P.
  2. Pick ST values: far below, each strike, each breakeven, far above.
  3. Each leg's profit: stock ST − S0 (long) or S0 − ST (short); options as in Session 15.
  4. Add across the row. Read max profit, max loss, and where the total crosses 0 (breakeven).
  5. Slope check: long stock +1 everywhere; long call +1 above K; short call −1 above K; long put −1 below K; short put +1 below K. Sum the slopes in each region to sketch the diagram.

Worked example — leverage: shares vs a call

Given: ₹1,000 stock. Choice A: buy 500 shares for ₹5,00,000. Choice B: buy 1 lot of the 1,000 call at ₹40 → 40 × 500 = ₹20,000.

STA: shares P&L (₹)A: returnB: call P&L (₹)B: return
1,100100 × 500 = 50,00010%(100 − 40) × 500 = 30,000150%

Interpretation: the call earns less in rupees but 15× the percentage return — and loses everything if the stock merely stays flat. Leverage cuts both ways.

Recap Profit = payoff ∓ premium. Build tables leg by leg, add across, read max/min/zero. Motives: speculate, bet on volatility, earn income, insure.

2 Long call & short call

Explain like I'm 5

Long call: you pay a car dealer ₹40 to lock today's price — if prices are hiked, you win. Short call: you are the dealer; you pocket the ₹40 but must sell at the old price however high the new one goes.

Long call / short call profitLong = max(ST − K, 0) − c · Short = c − max(ST − K, 0) · Breakeven = K + c

Common mistakes

(1) Saying the buyer "shouldn't exercise" between K and K + c — exercising still reduces the loss. (2) Calling the writer's risk "the premium" — the writer's gain is the premium; the loss is unlimited, so the broker blocks margin.

Recap Long call: lose c at most, gain without limit above K + c. Short call: the mirror — keep c at most, unlimited loss.

3 Long put & short put

Explain like I'm 5

Long put: insurance — if your bike's value crashes, the insurer buys it from you at the agreed value. Short put: you are the insurer; you collect the premium and hope nothing crashes.

Long put / short put profitLong = max(K − ST, 0) − p · Short = p − max(K − ST, 0) · Breakeven = K − p

Worked example — a short put as a "paid buy order"

You'd happily buy Masala Motors at ₹965. Write the 1,000 put for ₹35. If ST < 1,000 you are assigned: you buy at 1,000 but kept 35 → effective cost 1,000 − 35 = ₹965. If ST ≥ 1,000 you just keep ₹17,500 a lot. Keeping cash for the purchase ready (₹5,00,000) makes it a cash-secured put.

Recap Long put: lose p at most, gain up to K − p. Short put: keep p at most, lose up to K − p. Breakeven K − p for both.

4 Covered call & covered put

Explain like I'm 5

You own a flat and take ₹2 lakh today from someone for the right to buy it from you at a fixed higher price. If prices soar you must sell at that price; if not, you keep the flat and the ₹2 lakh. That is a covered call.

Covered call = long stock + short call

  • "Covered" because if the call is exercised you deliver shares you already own — no unlimited loss, unlike a naked call.
  • Usually an OTM call (K above S0): you give away gains above K in exchange for premium now.
Covered callProfit = (ST − S0) + c − max(ST − K, 0) · Max profit = (K − S0) + c · Max loss = S0 − c · BE = S0 − c
Worked example — own 500 shares at ₹1,000, write the 1,050 call at ₹20

Premium received = 20 × 500 = ₹10,000 = 2% of the ₹5,00,000 holding for one month.

  • Short call column: 20 − max(ST − 1,050, 0). It is +20 up to 1,050, then falls ₹1 for every ₹1 rise.
  • Below 1,050: call lapses; you keep the shares and the ₹20 — the premium cushions the first ₹20 of any fall. BE = 1,000 − 20 = ₹980.
  • Above 1,050: shares are "called away" at 1,050. Gain is frozen at (1,050 − 1,000) + 20 = ₹70 (₹35,000 a lot) — at 1,200 you miss ₹130 of upside.
  • Max loss = 1,000 − 20 = ₹980 a share (₹4,90,000) if the stock goes to 0 — almost the full stock risk.

When to use: you hold the stock for the long run and expect a flat-to-slightly-up month. Avoid before an expected rally (capped) — and it is no crash protection (only ₹20 cushion).

Covered put = short stock + short put

  • The exact mirror of the covered call, for someone who is already short the stock.
  • You write an OTM put (K below S0). "Covered" because if assigned you must buy shares at K — which you need anyway to close the short.
  • The short put adds income, not protection: if the stock rises, the short stock keeps losing and nothing caps it.
Covered putProfit = (S0 − ST) + p − max(K − ST, 0) · Max profit = (S0 − K) + p · Max loss = unlimited · BE = S0 + p

Indian practice: a short sale in the cash market must be delivered at settlement, so a multi-day short stock needs borrowed shares (SLB). Traders usually build the covered put with short stock futures + short put instead.

Common mistakes

(1) Mixing up the covered put (short stock + short put: income, unlimited loss) with the protective put (long stock + long put: insurance). (2) Thinking the covered put's short put protects against a rise — it only pays ₹15. (3) Some brokers loosely call a cash-secured short put a "covered put"; the textbook covered put is short stock + short put — use that unless the slides say otherwise.

Recap Covered call: own + write call → income, upside capped at (K − S0) + c, BE S0 − c. Covered put: short + write put → income, gain capped at (S0 − K) + p, BE S0 + p, loss unlimited.

5 Protective put & protective call

Explain like I'm 5

A protective put is health insurance for shares you own; a protective call is the same idea for a short seller, who is hurt when prices rise.

Protective put = long stock + long put (recap of Session 15)

Protective putProfit = (ST − S0) + max(K − ST, 0) − p · Max loss = (S0 − K) + p · Max profit = unlimited · BE = S0 + p

Reading it: the put column is max(950 − ST, 0) − 15. Below 950 the put gain exactly offsets further stock losses → floor of (1,000 − 950) + 15 = ₹65 (₹32,500 a lot). Above, you keep all upside minus ₹15. Every number is the covered-put table with the sign flipped — they are exact opposites.

Key takeaway — the mirror pairs

Covered call ↔ protective call and covered put ↔ protective put are exact opposites (same breakeven, max profit and max loss swapped). Learn two, get four.

Recap Protective put: long stock + long put, max loss (S0 − K) + p, BE S0 + p. Protective call: short stock + long call, max loss (K − S0) + c, BE S0 − c.

6 Spreads, straddles & strangles

Explain like I'm 5

A spread is buying a lottery ticket and selling off the jackpot above a level to make your ticket cheaper. A straddle is betting "something big happens on results day" without saying which way.

Bull call spread = buy lower-strike call + sell higher-strike call

Bull call spreadNet debit = cK1 − cK2 · Max profit = (K2 − K1) − debit · Max loss = debit · BE = K1 + debit
Worked example — buy 1,000 call @40, sell 1,100 call @12

Net debit = 40 − 12 = ₹28 (₹14,000 a lot) — 30% cheaper than the plain call.

Below 1,000 both lapse → lose the ₹28 debit. Between the strikes only the long call pays → total rises ₹1 per ₹1. Above 1,100 the short call cancels further gains → max (1,100 − 1,000) − 28 = ₹72. Reward : risk = 72 : 28 ≈ 2.6 : 1. View: moderately bullish, target ≈ 1,100.

Bear put spread = buy higher-strike put + sell lower-strike put

Bear put spreadNet debit = pK1 − pK2 (K1 > K2) · Max profit = (K1 − K2) − debit · Max loss = debit · BE = K1 − debit
Worked example — buy 1,000 put @35, sell 900 put @9

Long straddle = buy call + buy put, same strike

Long straddleCost = c + p · Max loss = c + p (at ST = K) · BEs = K − (c + p) and K + (c + p) · Max profit: unlimited up, K − (c + p) down
Worked example — results next week: buy 1,000 call @40 + 1,000 put @35

Cost = 40 + 35 = ₹75 (₹37,500 a lot). BEs = 1,000 ± 75 = 925 and 1,075.

Symmetric V: worst case the stock doesn't move (lose ₹75); profit needs a move of more than 7.5% either way. Risk: IV crush — after the event, implied volatility drops and both premiums can fall even if the stock moves a little.

Long strangle = buy OTM call + buy OTM put

Long strangleCost = cK2 + pK1 (K1 < K2) · Max loss = cost (K1 ≤ ST ≤ K2) · BEs = K1 − cost and K2 + cost
Worked example — buy 1,100 call @12 + 900 put @9

Cost = 12 + 9 = ₹21 (₹10,500 a lot). BEs = 900 − 21 = 879 and 1,100 + 21 = 1,121.

Short straddle (sell 1,000 call + 1,000 put): +₹75 if ST = 1,000, zero at 925 / 1,075, and at 1,200 a loss of 75 − 200 = −₹125 a share (−₹62,500 a lot) — growing without limit.

7 Choosing a strategy & the risks

Explain like I'm 5

Check two forecasts before you leave home: which way (rain or sun → direction) and how wild (storm or calm → volatility). The pair tells you whether to carry an umbrella, sunglasses, or both.

Strategy by market view and volatility view

Direction ↓ / Volatility →Expect a big move (σ ↑)Expect a moderate moveExpect calm (σ ↓)
BullishLong callBull call spreadShort put · covered call (if you own the stock)
Neutral / unsureLong straddle · long strangle—Short straddle / strangle (unlimited risk) · covered call / put for income
BearishLong putBear put spreadShort call · covered put (if you are short)
HedgingOwn the stock, fear a fall → protective put · Short the stock, fear a rise → protective call
Worked example — pick the strategy
  • You own 500 Masala Motors shares, expect a dull month. → Covered call at 1,050: collect ₹10,000 (2%) now; max ₹35,000 if the shares are called away at 1,050.
  • Quarterly results tomorrow, big move expected, direction unknown. → Long straddle: risk ₹37,500, profit if the stock ends outside 925–1,075.
  • Mildly bullish, target 1,100, small budget. → Bull call spread: risk ₹14,000 to make up to ₹36,000.
  • Holding the stock, fear a crash but want upside. → Protective put at 950: worst case −₹32,500, upside open.

Risks you must state in an answer

(1) Unlimited-loss positions: short call, short straddle/strangle, covered put — one gap-up can wipe out months of premium. (2) Margin calls on written options. (3) Time decay works against every bought option. (4) Leverage: a flat market sends a bought option to −100%. (5) Costs (brokerage, STT, GST, stamp duty) shift every breakeven. (6) SEBI's own studies (2023, 2024) found roughly 9 in 10 individual F&O traders lost money.

Recap Choose on two axes: direction (bull/neutral/bear) and volatility (big move/calm). Know each strategy's max profit, max loss and breakeven cold — the summary table is the exam answer key.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Every strategy is a sum of four building blocks — long/short calls and puts — plus long or short stock. Build the payoff table leg by leg, add across, and the max profit, max loss and breakeven fall straight out. Bought options are bets on direction or volatility with a capped loss; written options earn premium for taking risk. Covered calls and covered puts add income to an existing long or short stock position by giving away part of the upside (or downside) move; protective puts and calls do the opposite, paying premium to cap the loss. Spreads make directional bets cheaper by capping both ends, and straddles and strangles profit from big moves either way. Pick by direction view × volatility view, and never forget which positions carry unlimited loss.

Practice & self-check

You buy a ₹500 call at ₹25 (lot 1,000, illustrative). Breakeven? Profit if ST = ₹560? If ST = ₹510, should you exercise?

BE = 500 + 25 = ₹525. At 560: (60 − 25) × 1,000 = ₹35,000. At 510: yes — payoff 10 → profit −15 × 1,000 = −₹15,000, better than −₹25,000 by letting it lapse.

You write a ₹800 put at ₹30. Breakeven, max profit, max loss, and profit at ST = ₹740?

BE = 800 − 30 = ₹770. Max profit = ₹30 (ST ≥ 800). Max loss = 800 − 30 = ₹770 (ST = 0). At 740: 30 − (800 − 740) = −₹30.

Covered call: own a stock bought at ₹2,000, write the ₹2,100 call at ₹45. Max profit, breakeven, and profit at ₹1,900 and ₹2,300?

Max profit = (2,100 − 2,000) + 45 = ₹145. BE = 2,000 − 45 = ₹1,955. At 1,900: −100 + 45 = −₹55. At 2,300: +300 + 45 − 200 = ₹145 (capped).

Covered put: short a stock at ₹600, write the ₹560 put at ₹12. Max profit, breakeven, and profit at ₹500 and ₹700?

Max profit = (600 − 560) + 12 = ₹52. BE = 600 + 12 = ₹612. At 500: +100 + 12 − 60 = ₹52. At 700: −100 + 12 = −₹88 (put lapses; loss unlimited as the price rises).

Bull call spread: buy the ₹300 call at ₹14, sell the ₹330 call at ₹5. Net debit, max profit, max loss, breakeven?

Debit = 14 − 5 = ₹9 = max loss. Max profit = (330 − 300) − 9 = ₹21 (ST ≥ 330). BE = 300 + 9 = ₹309.

Long straddle at K = ₹1,500 with call ₹60 and put ₹55. Breakevens? Profit at ₹1,700 and at ₹1,500?

Cost = 115. BEs = 1,500 ± 115 = ₹1,385 and ₹1,615. At 1,700: 200 − 115 = ₹85. At 1,500: both lapse → −₹115 (max loss).

Covered call vs protective put: which caps the upside, which floors the downside, and which suits a long-term holder who fears bad results next week?

Covered call caps the upside at K and gives only a premium-sized cushion; protective put floors the downside at (S0 − K) + p and keeps the upside. Fearing bad results → protective put.

Why is a covered put's loss unlimited even though it is called "covered"?

"Covered" only means the short put is matched by the short stock (if assigned, the shares bought at K close the short). Nothing protects the short stock from a rise: above S0 + p it loses ₹1 per ₹1, with no limit. To cap that you'd need a long call (protective call).
