1 Absorption vs marginal costing

Explain like I'm 5

A bakery's oven rent can be counted two ways. Absorption puts a slice of rent into every loaf, so unsold loaves "store" some rent for next month. Marginal charges the whole month's rent to this month; loaves carry only flour, butter and gas.

  • Absorption costing (full / traditional costing): both fixed and variable production overheads are included in product cost; fixed production overheads are absorbed into the unit cost (via the OAR, Session 5).
  • Marginal costing (variable costing): the profit statement separates variable costs from fixed costs, not by function. Fixed costs are period costs charged in full to the period, not spread over units.

2 CVP analysis, contribution & P/V ratio

Explain like I'm 5

A kulhad chai sells for ₹25 and uses ₹10 of milk, tea, sugar and the clay cup. The ₹15 left from every cup first pays the stall's rent and helper's salary; only after those are covered is it profit.

Cost–Volume–Profit (CVP) analysis is a management accounting technique to understand how changes in costs, sales volume and selling price affect profit. It rests on splitting costs into fixed and variable. It answers:

  • How many units must we sell to break even? … to earn a target profit?
  • What happens to profit if volume, selling price, variable cost or fixed cost changes?
  • How far can sales fall before we make a loss?
TermMeaning (slide)
Selling price (SP)Revenue per unit
Variable cost (VC)Cost per unit that changes with volume
Contribution margin (CM)SP − VC — what each unit contributes to fixed costs and then profit
Fixed costs (FC)Costs that don't change with volume (rent, salaries…)
Break-even point (BEP)Volume at which total revenue = total cost → profit = 0

Key formulae 1–5 with the slide examples

1. Sales revenueSales Revenue = Selling Price per Unit × Units Sold = ₹2,000 × 5,000 = ₹1,00,00,000
2. Total variable costTVC = Variable Cost per Unit (V) × Number of Units (Q)

Slide: material ₹1,200 + labour ₹500 + variable overhead ₹300 = V ₹2,000; Q = 10,000 → TVC = ₹2,00,00,000.

3. ContributionContribution = Sales − Variable Cost  ·  Total Contribution = (SP per unit − VC per unit) × Units Sold = Contribution per Unit × Units Sold

Slide: SP ₹500, VC ₹300 → CM = ₹200/unit; 10,000 units → total contribution = ₹20,00,000.

4. ProfitProfit = Total Contribution − Total Fixed Cost

With the slide's fixed costs of ₹12,00,000 (used on its target-profit slide): 20,00,000 − 12,00,000 = ₹8,00,000.

5. P/V ratioP/V Ratio = (Contribution ÷ Sales) × 100 = (₹200 ÷ ₹500) × 100 = 40%

The P/V ratio (profit–volume ratio, = contribution margin ratio) is contribution from every ₹1 of sales: ₹100 of sales → ₹40 of contribution. It is the same per unit or in total (20,00,000 ÷ 50,00,000 = 40%).

The marginal cost equation

Marginal cost equationS − V = F + P  ⇔  Contribution = Fixed cost + Profit
SymbolMeaning
STotal sales (₹)
VTotal variable cost (₹)
FTotal fixed cost (₹)
PProfit (negative = loss). At BEP, P = 0 so contribution = F.

SP ₹25/cup; VC ₹10/cup; fixed costs ₹45,000 (rent 25,000 + helper 18,000 + licence & misc 2,000). Sold 4,200 cups.

Marginal cost equation check: S − V = 1,05,000 − 42,000 = 63,000 = F + P = 45,000 + 18,000 ✓.

P/V ratio from two periods (when costs aren't split)

P/V ratio — two periodsP/V Ratio = (Change in Profit ÷ Change in Sales) × 100

Works because fixed cost is the same in both periods, so every extra rupee of profit came from contribution.

Worked example — Rangoli Textiles (fictional)

Period 1: sales ₹40,00,000, profit ₹4,00,000. Period 2: sales ₹50,00,000, profit ₹7,00,000.

  1. P/V = (7,00,000 − 4,00,000) ÷ (50,00,000 − 40,00,000) = 3,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 = 30% (0.30).
  2. Fixed cost = Contribution − Profit = 40,00,000 × 0.30 − 4,00,000 = 12,00,000 − 4,00,000 = ₹8,00,000 (check period 2: 15,00,000 − 7,00,000 = 8,00,000 ✓).
  3. Profit at ₹60,00,000 sales = 60,00,000 × 0.30 − 8,00,000 = ₹10,00,000.
  4. BEP sales (§3) = 8,00,000 ÷ 0.30 = ₹26,66,667 (rounded).
Recap Contribution = sales − variable cost; profit = contribution − fixed cost; P/V = contribution ÷ sales (or Δprofit ÷ Δsales). S − V = F + P is the one equation behind every CVP formula.

3 Break-even point & the break-even chart

Explain like I'm 5

Each chai earns ₹15 towards the ₹45,000 monthly bills. 45,000 ÷ 15 = 3,000 cups — the 3,001st cup is the first one that puts money in the owner's pocket.

Break-even analysis finds the sales level at which total revenue = total cost; the business makes neither profit nor loss.

6. BEP in unitsBEP (Units) = Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution per Unit
6. BEP in sales valueBEP (Sales ₹) = Fixed Cost ÷ P/V Ratio  (= BEP units × SP)
Worked example — slide: BEP in sales value

Fixed cost ₹2,00,000; P/V ratio 40% (0.40). BEP sales = 2,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = ₹5,00,000. At ₹5 lakh of sales, contribution = 5,00,000 × 0.40 = ₹2,00,000 = fixed cost → profit 0 ✓.

Worked example — slide illustration (shown in $ on the slide; same arithmetic in ₹)

SP ₹50/unit, VC ₹30/unit, fixed costs ₹40,000. CM = 50 − 30 = ₹20/unit; P/V = 20 ÷ 50 = 40%.

BEP (units) = 40,000 ÷ 20 = 2,000 units; BEP (sales value) = 2,000 × 50 = ₹1,00,000 (= 40,000 ÷ 0.40 ✓).

Worked example — Kulhad Chai Co. break-even

CM ₹15, P/V 60%, FC ₹45,000. BEP = 45,000 ÷ 15 = 3,000 cups/month = 100 cups a day (30-day month). BEP sales = 45,000 ÷ 0.60 = ₹75,000 (= 3,000 × 25 ✓).

Decision use: if the street sees only ~80 buyers a day, this location can never break even at ₹25 — change price, costs or location.

Common mistakes
  • Dividing fixed cost by selling price instead of contribution per unit.
  • Writing P/V as 40 instead of 0.40 when dividing: 2,00,000 ÷ 40 = ₹5,000 (wrong) vs ÷ 0.40 = ₹5,00,000.
  • Rounding BEP units down: 7,058.8 units → you need 7,059 to cover fixed cost.
Recap BEP units = FC ÷ CM per unit; BEP ₹ = FC ÷ P/V ratio. On the chart, BEP is where the revenue line crosses the total-cost line; the vertical gap is profit or loss.

4 Margin of safety & target profit

Explain like I'm 5

If the chai stall needs 3,000 cups to break even and sells 4,200, it has a 1,200-cup cushion: a rainy month can knock off 1,200 cups before it starts losing money.

Margin of safety (MoS) = current (actual) sales − break-even sales: how much sales can fall before the company drops below BEP. Higher MoS → lower risk of loss.

7. MoS in unitsMoS (Units) = Actual Units Sold − Break-Even Units
8. MoS in value and %MoS (Value) = Actual Sales − Break-Even Sales  ·  MoS % = (MoS ÷ Actual Sales) × 100
Link to profitProfit = MoS (₹) × P/V Ratio  ·  MoS (₹) = Profit ÷ P/V Ratio

Why: every rupee of sales above BEP earns P/V of contribution, and fixed costs are already covered at BEP.

Worked examples — slides
  • Sells 10,000 units, BEP 6,000 units → MoS = 10,000 − 6,000 = 4,000 units — the cushion against a fall in volume.
  • Actual sales ₹10,00,000, BEP sales ₹6,00,000 → MoS = ₹4,00,000; MoS % = 4,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = 40% → sales can fall 40% before reaching BEP.
Worked example — slide illustration extended to 3,000 units
  • Revenue = 3,000 × 50 = ₹1,50,000; total cost = 40,000 + 3,000 × 30 = ₹1,30,000.
  • Profit = 1,50,000 − 1,30,000 = ₹20,000.
  • MoS = 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000 units = ₹50,000 of sales; MoS ratio = 50,000 ÷ 1,50,000 = 33.3% — sales could drop by a third before a loss.
  • Check: MoS × P/V = 50,000 × 0.40 = ₹20,000 = profit ✓.
Worked example — Kulhad Chai Co. MoS

Actual 4,200 cups, BEP 3,000 → MoS = 1,200 cups = ₹30,000; MoS % = 1,200 ÷ 4,200 = 28.57%. Profit = 30,000 × 0.60 = ₹18,000 — same as the contribution statement in §2 ✓.

Volume for a target profit

9. Required sales for target profitRequired Sales Volume (Units) = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per Unit  ·  In ₹ = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ P/V Ratio
Worked example — slide: target profit ₹8,00,000

SP ₹500, VC ₹300, FC ₹12,00,000. CM = ₹200.

Units = (12,00,000 + 8,00,000) ÷ 200 = 20,00,000 ÷ 200 = 10,000 units. In ₹: 20,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = ₹50,00,000 (= 10,000 × 500 ✓).

After-tax target: if the ₹8 lakh is wanted after 25% tax, pre-tax target = 8,00,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = ₹10,66,667, so units = (12,00,000 + 10,66,667) ÷ 200 = 11,333.3 → 11,334 units. (Conversely PAT ₹6,00,000 at 25% tax = PBT ₹8,00,000 → 10,000 units.)

Worked example — Kulhad Chai Co. wants ₹30,000 a month

Cups = (45,000 + 30,000) ÷ 15 = 5,000 cups/month → 5,000 ÷ 30 = 166.7 → 167 cups a day. Sales needed = 75,000 ÷ 0.60 = ₹1,25,000.

Glimpse — operating leverage (not on the slides): degree of operating leverage = contribution ÷ profit = 1 ÷ MoS ratio. At 3,000 units above: 60,000 ÷ 20,000 = 3 → a 10% rise in volume (3,300 units) lifts profit 30%, to ₹26,000. Low MoS = high leverage = profit swings hard.

Common mistake

Dividing an after-tax target by CM directly. Gross it up first: pre-tax profit = after-tax profit ÷ (1 − tax rate). Fixed costs are covered before tax, never "after tax".

Recap MoS = actual − BEP (units, ₹ or %); profit = MoS ₹ × P/V. Target volume = (FC + target profit) ÷ CM — gross up after-tax targets first.

5 What-if analysis & CVP assumptions

Explain like I'm 5

Cutting the chai price by ₹2 takes ₹2 off every cup's ₹15 cushion; a ₹2,000 rise in rent only moves the finish line a little further away. Same rupees, very different damage.

Base case (the slides' product): SP ₹500, VC ₹300, FC ₹12,00,000, volume 10,000 → CM ₹200, P/V 40%, BEP 6,000 units, profit ₹8,00,000, MoS 40%. Change one thing at a time:

Working, e.g. price cut: CM = 450 − 300 = 150; BEP = 12,00,000 ÷ 150 = 8,000; profit = 150 × 10,000 − 12,00,000 = ₹3,00,000. VC rise: BEP = 12,00,000 ÷ 170 = 7,058.8 → 7,059 units.

  • Price change hits CM by the full amount → moves P/V, BEP and profit most (−₹50 on a ₹200 CM = −25% of CM).
  • VC change also moves CM and P/V, but ₹30 here vs ₹50 for the price cut.
  • FC change moves BEP and profit, never CM or P/V.
  • Volume change moves profit and MoS, never BEP.
  • To keep ₹8 lakh profit after the 10% price cut: (12,00,000 + 8,00,000) ÷ 150 = 13,333.3 → 13,334 units (+33.3% volume). A 10% discount needs a one-third volume jump just to stand still.

CVP assumptions and limitations

AssumptionWhy it can fail in reality
Costs can be split cleanly into fixed and variableSemi-variable and step costs need estimates (high-low, Session 5)
SP and VC per unit are constant (straight lines)Bulk discounts to customers, volume discounts from suppliers, overtime
Fixed costs are constantOnly within the relevant range; beyond capacity they step up

Recap Price and VC changes move CM and P/V; FC changes move BEP; volume changes move profit and MoS. CVP is linear and short-run — use it within the relevant range.

6 Make or buy & special orders

Explain like I'm 5

Should the hostel mess roll its own rotis or buy them? Compare the supplier's price only with the costs that would actually disappear if the mess stopped making them — the kitchen rent stays either way.

The rule for every decision below: compare only relevant costs and revenues (Session 5) — variable costs, avoidable fixed costs and opportunity costs. Apportioned fixed costs that stay anyway are irrelevant.

Make or buy

Qualitative checks: supplier quality and reliability, protecting design secrets, dependence on one vendor, idle workers, the chance to use the capacity for something better.

Accept or reject a special order

  • Spare capacity: accept if price > marginal cost + any order-specific costs (incremental contribution > 0).
  • No spare capacity: also charge the contribution lost on regular sales displaced (opportunity cost) — the airline rule from Session 5.
  • Also check: one-off order, separate market (won't drag down regular prices), customer won't expect the price again.
Worked example — Kanpur Leather Works (fictional), bags

Capacity 10,000 bags/month; current sales 7,000 at ₹1,500. Variable cost ₹900 (DM 500 + DL 250 + variable OH 150). Fixed costs ₹21,00,000/month (= ₹300 per bag at 7,000) → full cost ₹1,200. Current profit = 7,000 × (1,500 − 900) − 21,00,000 = ₹21,00,000.

An exporter offers ₹1,000 per bag (below full cost!). Extra export packaging ₹50/bag; one-off certification ₹40,000.

Common mistakes
  • Comparing the supplier's price (or the special-order price) with full cost including apportioned fixed costs that won't change.
  • Forgetting order-specific fixed costs (certification, tooling) or the contribution lost when capacity runs out.
Recap Make or buy: buy only if the price beats variable + avoidable fixed + opportunity cost. Special order: accept if incremental contribution (after specific costs and displaced sales) is positive.

Explain like I'm 5

If your oven has only 9 hours a day, bake whatever earns the most per oven-hour — not whatever earns the most per tray.

Papad "loses" ₹1.5 lakh. Drop it?

  • If all ₹30 lakh fixed cost stays: new profit = (16 + 14) lakh − 30 lakh = ₹0 — profit falls by Papad's full ₹6 lakh contribution. Keep Papad.
  • If ₹2 lakh of fixed cost is avoidable (its packing staff): new profit = 30 − 28 = ₹2 lakh — still ₹4 lakh worse. Keep.
  • If the freed capacity could make a new line with ₹9 lakh contribution: 16 + 14 + 9 − 30 = ₹9 lakh > ₹6 lakh → replace Papad.

Rule: drop a line only if its avoidable fixed costs exceed its contribution, or its capacity can earn more elsewhere.

Temporary shut-down point

Shut-down pointShut-down point (units) = (Fixed cost if operating − Fixed cost still incurred if shut) ÷ Contribution per unit = Avoidable fixed cost ÷ CM
Worked example — slack season

Fixed costs ₹10,00,000 a quarter when running; ₹6,00,000 still payable if the plant is closed for the quarter (rent, security, key staff). CM ₹200/unit. Shut-down point = 4,00,000 ÷ 200 = 2,000 units.

  • Expected demand 1,500: operate → 1,500 × 200 − 10,00,000 = −₹7,00,000; close → −₹6,00,000 → close.
  • Expected demand 2,500: operate → −₹5,00,000; close → −₹6,00,000 → keep running, even at a loss.

Key takeaway

Every decision on this page is the same question: what is the change in total contribution, after avoidable fixed costs and opportunity costs? Apportioned fixed costs that don't change never decide anything.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Once costs are split into fixed and variable, every unit sold earns a contribution (SP − VC) that first pays fixed costs and then becomes profit. That one idea gives the break-even point (FC ÷ CM), the margin of safety (how far above BEP you are), the volume for any target profit, and instant what-if answers for price, cost and volume changes. Marginal costing formalises it as a profit statement, while absorption costing (needed for published accounts) parks some fixed cost in stock. For decisions — make or buy, special orders, dropping a line, product mix under a bottleneck, price floors — compare only the change in contribution after avoidable fixed and opportunity costs.

Practice & self-check

SP ₹800, VC ₹480, fixed costs ₹9,60,000, sales 4,000 units. Find CM, P/V ratio, BEP (units and ₹), profit and margin of safety.

CM = 800 − 480 = ₹320; P/V = 320 ÷ 800 = 40%. BEP = 9,60,000 ÷ 320 = 3,000 units = 9,60,000 ÷ 0.40 = ₹24,00,000. Profit = 4,000 × 320 − 9,60,000 = ₹3,20,000. MoS = 1,000 units = ₹8,00,000 = 25%; check 8,00,000 × 0.40 = ₹3,20,000 ✓.

Sales ₹30 lakh → profit ₹3 lakh; sales ₹36 lakh → profit ₹4.8 lakh. Find P/V ratio, fixed cost, BEP and MoS at ₹36 lakh.

P/V = (4,80,000 − 3,00,000) ÷ (36,00,000 − 30,00,000) = 1,80,000 ÷ 6,00,000 = 30%. FC = 30,00,000 × 0.30 − 3,00,000 = ₹6,00,000. BEP = 6,00,000 ÷ 0.30 = ₹20,00,000. MoS = 36 − 20 = ₹16,00,000 (44.44%); check 16,00,000 × 0.30 = ₹4,80,000 ✓.

FC ₹12,00,000, CM ₹200/unit. Management wants ₹4,50,000 profit after 25% tax. How many units?

Pre-tax profit = 4,50,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = ₹6,00,000. Units = (12,00,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ 200 = 9,000 units.

Fixed production OH ₹4,00,000; normal output 8,000 units. Produced 8,000, sold 7,000, no opening stock. Which method shows higher profit, and by how much?

Fixed OH per unit = 4,00,000 ÷ 8,000 = ₹50. Stock rises by 1,000 units → absorption profit is higher by 1,000 × 50 = ₹50,000 (that fixed OH sits in closing stock instead of being expensed).

A part costs ₹75 variable + ₹25 apportioned fixed to make; a supplier offers ₹90. Need 10,000 units. Make or buy? What if buying frees capacity that could earn ₹1,20,000 contribution?

Fixed ₹25 is unavoidable → relevant make cost ₹75 < ₹90 → make (saves 10,000 × 15 = ₹1,50,000). With the opportunity: make = 7,50,000 + 1,20,000 = ₹8,70,000 vs buy ₹9,00,000 → still make, saving ₹30,000.

Labour hours limited to 12,000. Product A: CM ₹60, 3 h/unit, demand 3,000. Product B: CM ₹80, 5 h/unit, demand 2,000. Best mix and contribution?

CM per hour: A = 60 ÷ 3 = ₹20, B = 80 ÷ 5 = ₹16 → A first. A: 3,000 units × 3 = 9,000 h → ₹1,80,000. B: remaining 3,000 h ÷ 5 = 600 units → ₹48,000. Total ₹2,28,000. (B first would give 2,000 × 80 + 666 × 60 = ₹1,99,960.)

A product line shows a loss of ₹1 lakh after apportioned fixed costs, but its contribution is ₹3 lakh. Should it be dropped?

Not if the fixed costs stay: dropping it loses ₹3 lakh of contribution, so total profit falls by ₹3 lakh (minus any fixed costs actually avoided). Drop only if avoidable fixed costs > ₹3 lakh or the capacity can earn more than ₹3 lakh elsewhere.

Name four assumptions of CVP analysis.

(1) Costs split cleanly into fixed and variable; (2) SP and VC per unit constant (linear); (3) fixed costs constant within the relevant range; (4) production = sales (no stock change); also single product / constant sales mix and volume as the only cost driver.
