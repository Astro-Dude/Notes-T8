2 Liquidity ratios

Explain like I'm 5

Your phone EMI, rent and mess bill are due this month. Liquidity is whether your wallet, bank balance and money friends owe you add up to more than those bills.

  • Liquidity ratios measure a company's ability to meet its short-term financial obligations as and when they become due.
  • Slide question: "Does the business have enough short-term assets and cash-generating capacity to pay its short-term liabilities?"
Short-term liabilities (payable within one year)Short-term assets available to pay them
Trade payables / creditors · short-term borrowings · bank overdrafts · accrued expenses · outstanding wages & salaries · taxes payable · current portion of long-term debtCash & cash equivalents · bank balances · short-term investments / marketable securities · trade receivables · inventory · other current assets

Current ratio

Relationship between current assets and current liabilities: the extent to which current assets cover current liabilities.

Current RatioCurrent Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Current assets = cash & bank, inventories, trade receivables, prepaid expenses, short-term investments. Current liabilities = trade payables, bank overdraft, short-term borrowings, accrued expenses and other short-term obligations.

Quick (acid-test) ratio

Can the company meet current liabilities from its most liquid current assets, without relying on selling inventory? Inventory may take time to sell and may not convert into cash easily.

Quick RatioQuick Ratio = Quick Assets / Current Liabilities, where Quick Assets = Current Assets − Inventory

Some textbooks also deduct prepaid expenses; the class definition deducts only inventory.

Cash ratio

The strictest test: only cash, cash equivalents and highly liquid marketable securities.

Cash RatioCash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Marketable Securities) / Current Liabilities

Common mistakes
  • Window dressing: if Tiffinwala uses its ₹1,00,00,000 cash to pay suppliers on 31 March, CA = 4,50,00,000 and CL = 2,37,50,000 → current ratio rises to 1.89, while the cash ratio falls to 30,00,000 / 2,37,50,000 = 0.13. When CR > 1, paying off current liabilities raises it.
  • Leaving short-term borrowings or the current portion of long-term debt out of current liabilities.
  • Subtracting inventory for the current ratio — that's the quick ratio.
  • Treating "higher is always better": a current ratio of 4 often means money is stuck in stock or debtors.
Recap Current = CA/CL; Quick = (CA − Inventory)/CL; Cash = (Cash + equivalents + marketable securities)/CL. Tiffinwala: 1.63, 1.16, 0.39 — comfortable.

3 Efficiency (turnover) ratios

Explain like I'm 5

A sabzi-wala who sells out his cart every evening turns his stock 365 times a year; a jeweller may turn hers twice. Turnover = how many times a rupee tied up in stock, debtors or assets comes back as sales in a year.

  • Efficiency ratios (also activity or asset-management ratios) measure how effectively a business uses its assets and working capital to generate sales.
  • Slide question: "How efficiently is the company using the resources available to it to generate revenue?" — resources = inventory, trade receivables, working capital, fixed assets, total assets.
  • Each turnover ratio has a "days" twin: days = 365 / turnover.

Inventory turnover & inventory holding period

How many times a company sells and replaces its inventory in a period; the holding period (Days Inventory Outstanding) is the average number of days stock stays before being sold.

Inventory Turnover RatioInventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory
Average InventoryAverage Inventory = (Opening Inventory + Closing Inventory) / 2
Inventory Holding PeriodInventory Holding Period = Average Inventory / COGS × 365

Receivables turnover & average collection period

How efficiently the company collects from customers; the collection period (Days Sales Outstanding, debtor days) is the average number of days customers take to pay.

Receivables Turnover RatioReceivables Turnover = Net Credit Sales / Average Trade Receivables
Average Trade ReceivablesAverage Trade Receivables = (Opening Receivables + Closing Receivables) / 2
Average Collection PeriodAverage Collection Period = Average Trade Receivables / Net Credit Sales × 365

Payables turnover & average payment period

How efficiently the company pays its suppliers; the payment period (Days Payable Outstanding, payables days) is the average number of days it takes to pay them. If credit purchases are unavailable, use COGS.

Payables Turnover RatioPayables Turnover = Credit Purchases / Average Trade Payables
Average Trade PayablesAverage Trade Payables = (Opening Payables + Closing Payables) / 2
Average Payment PeriodAverage Payment Period = Average Trade Payables / Credit Purchases × 365

Common mistakes
  • Inventory turnover with sales instead of COGS — inventory is carried at cost, so divide cost by cost.
  • Receivables turnover with total sales when credit sales are given (cash sales never create receivables).
  • Using closing balances when opening balances are given — the slides use averages.
  • Mixing 360 and 365 days within one answer (the slides use 365).
Recap Inventory: COGS/avg inventory (8.00x, 45.6 days). Receivables: credit sales/avg receivables (9.00x, 40.6 days). Payables: credit purchases/avg payables (8.11x, 45.0 days). Days = 365/turnover.

4 Working capital (operating) cycle

Explain like I'm 5

Count the days from paying the dal supplier to getting money from the shop that bought your tiffins. For those days your own money is locked up — the shorter, the better.

The working capital cycle (also operating cycle) measures the time a business takes to convert money invested in day-to-day operations back into cash.

Operating Cycle (class formula)Operating Cycle = Inventory Holding Period + Receivable Days − Payable Days
TermMeaning
Inventory Holding Period (DIO)Days stock waits before being sold
Receivable Days (DSO)Days customers take to pay after the sale
Payable Days (DPO)Days the company takes to pay its suppliers (supplier credit shortens the cash gap)
Terminology

Many textbooks call DIO + DSO the gross operating cycle and DIO + DSO − DPO the net operating cycle or cash conversion cycle (CCC). The class formula is the net version; use it in exams and mention CCC as the alternative name.

Worked example — Tiffinwala FY26 
Day              0                          45                       86.2
Stock on shelf   |==========================|                           inventory holding 45.6 days
Supplier credit  |==========================|                           payment period 45.0 days
Customer owes                               |========================|  collection period 40.6 days
Own cash locked                             |========================|  operating cycle 41.2 days
(scale ≈ 1.7 days per character, so day 45.0 and day 45.6 share a tick)
  1. Operating cycle = 45.625 + 40.556 − 45.000 = 41.2 days (gross cycle 45.6 + 40.6 = 86.2 days).
  2. One day of credit sales = 18,00,00,000 / 365 = ₹4,93,151.
  3. If collection days fall by 10 (40.6 → 30.6), average receivables fall by 10 × 4,93,151 ≈ ₹49,31,507 (to ₹1,50,68,493) and the cycle shortens to 31.2 days.

Interpretation: Tiffinwala's own money (or its cash-credit loan) funds every rupee for ~41 days. Collecting 10 days faster frees ≈ ₹49 lakh — enough to repay half the ₹1 crore short-term loan and cut interest.

Levers to shorten the cycle

ComponentLeverTrade-off
Inventory days ↓Better demand forecasting, smaller batches, just-in-time buyingStock-out risk

Common mistakes
  • Adding payable days instead of subtracting them.
  • Computing the three "days" with different day counts or with closing instead of average balances.
  • Calling a long cycle "good" because it means "lots of working capital" — a long cycle means cash is locked up and must be financed.
Recap Operating cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days. Tiffinwala: 45.6 + 40.6 − 45.0 = 41.2 days. Each day cut releases one day of sales (or cost) in cash.

5 Return ratios & DuPont analysis

Explain like I'm 5

A ₹1,00,000 fixed deposit paying ₹7,000 a year is a 7% return. Return ratios ask the same of a business: how many rupees of profit for every rupee put in by owners and lenders?

  • Return ratios measure profit relative to capital, assets or equity (margins in Session 3 were profit relative to revenue).
  • Why both? The slides list traps: strong liquidity but poor profitability · high sales but low margins · high profits but poor returns on capital · high returns because of excessive financial leverage. Profit must be judged against what was invested.

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

How efficiently the company generates operating profit from the long-term capital employed (from owners and lenders).

ROCEROCE = EBIT / Capital Employed × 100
Capital EmployedCapital Employed = Total Assets − Current Liabilities = Equity + Long-term Debt (alternative)

Return on Assets (ROA)

ROAROA = Profit After Tax / Average Total Assets × 100, where Average Assets = (Opening Assets + Closing Assets) / 2

Return on Equity (ROE)

Profit generated from the equity capital invested by shareholders.

ROEROE = Profit After Tax / Average Shareholders' Equity × 100

Shareholders' equity = share capital + retained earnings (undistributed profits belonging to shareholders).

Worked example — Tiffinwala FY26 returns (2 dp)
StepSubstitutionResult
Capital employed (method 1)17,50,00,000 − 3,37,50,000₹14,12,50,000
Capital employed (method 2)10,12,50,000 + 4,00,00,000₹14,12,50,000 ✔
ROCE3,00,00,000 / 14,12,50,000 × 10021.24%
ROA1,87,50,000 / 15,75,00,000 × 10011.90%
ROE1,87,50,000 / 9,56,25,000 × 10019.61%

Interpretation: every ₹100 of long-term capital earns ₹21.24 of operating profit, well above the ~12.5% interest Tiffinwala pays (50,00,000 / average debt 4,00,00,000). Because borrowed money earns more than it costs, shareholders get 19.61% — higher than the 11.90% the assets earn overall. That gap is leverage at work (Session 3 §4).

  • The two capital-employed methods agree here because Tiffinwala's only non-current liability is its term loan. If a firm also has deferred tax or long-term provisions, method 1 includes them and method 2 doesn't — state your method.
  • The ROCE slide uses capital employed without averaging (closing). Using average CE ((11,50,00,000 + 14,12,50,000) / 2 = 12,81,25,000) gives 23.41%. Be consistent across years.

Return on Investment (ROI)

A broader measure of the return on a particular investment. Unlike ROE or ROCE there is no single universally accepted accounting formula; the class form:

ROIROI = (Gain from Investment − Investment Cost) / Investment Cost × 100
Worked example — two ROIs
  1. Marketing campaign: Tiffinwala spends ₹20,00,000 on a quick-commerce campaign that brings ₹32,00,000 of extra gross profit. ROI = (32,00,000 − 20,00,000) / 20,00,000 × 100 = 60%.
  2. Investor: buys 1,000 Tiffinwala shares at ₹48 (cost ₹48,000), receives dividend ₹1.50 × 1,000 = ₹1,500, sells at ₹60 (₹60,000). Gain = 60,000 + 1,500 = ₹61,500. ROI = (61,500 − 48,000) / 48,000 × 100 = 28.13% (28.125%).

Interpretation: both beat a ~7% fixed deposit — but ROI ignores time: 28% earned over one year is far better than over three years. Time value comes in Session 7.

DuPont analysis — why is ROE what it is?

DuPont analysis splits ROE into three drivers: profitability, efficiency and leverage.

DuPont identity (3-step)ROE = (PAT / Revenue) × (Revenue / Avg Total Assets) × (Avg Total Assets / Avg Equity) = NPM × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
DriverFormulaMeasuresLever
Net profit margin (NPM)PAT / RevenueProfitabilityPricing, cost control
Asset turnover (AT)Revenue / Average total assetsEfficiencySweat the assets, cut idle stock
Equity multiplier (EM)Average total assets / Average equityFinancial leverageMore debt (more risk)
Worked example — Tiffinwala FY26 DuPont (4 dp)
  1. NPM = 1,87,50,000 / 20,00,00,000 = 0.09375 (9.375%)
  2. Asset turnover = 20,00,00,000 / 15,75,00,000 = 1.2698x
  3. Equity multiplier = 15,75,00,000 / 9,56,25,000 = 1.6471x
  4. ROE = 0.09375 × 1.2698 × 1.6471 = 0.1961 = 19.61% ✔ matches the direct ROE.
  5. Two-step view: ROA = NPM × AT = 0.09375 × 1.2698 = 11.90%; ROE = ROA × EM = 11.90% × 1.6471 = 19.61%.

What-if: if Tiffinwala restores last year's 11.25% NPM with the same turnover and leverage, ROE = 0.1125 × 1.2698 × 1.6471 = 23.53%. The fastest route to a better ROE is fixing margin, not more debt.

Worked example 2 — same ROE, three different businesses (illustrative)
BusinessNPMAsset turnoverEquity multiplierROAROE
Luxury watch brand20.0%0.5x2.0x10%20%
Grocery chain2.5%4.0x2.0x10%20%

Interpretation: the watch brand earns ROE through margin, the grocer through turnover, the lender through leverage. The lender's 20% is the riskiest — a small fall in asset values wipes out thin equity.

Common mistakes
  • Mismatching numerator and denominator: ROCE uses EBIT (return to all capital); ROE uses PAT (return to shareholders). Never EBIT/equity or PAT/capital employed.
  • Mixing average and closing balances inside one DuPont calculation — then the three factors won't multiply back to ROE.
  • Celebrating a high ROE without checking the equity multiplier — it may be borrowed.
  • ROE is meaningless when equity is negative (accumulated losses).
Recap ROCE = EBIT/CE (21.24%); ROA = PAT/avg TA (11.90%); ROE = PAT/avg equity (19.61%); ROI = (gain − cost)/cost. DuPont: ROE = NPM × AT × EM = 9.375% × 1.2698 × 1.6471.

6 Market / investor ratios

Explain like I'm 5

EPS is your slice of the year's profit pizza for each share you own. P/E is how many years of today's slice a buyer is paying for when buying the share at today's price.

  • Market / investor ratios evaluate the company from the viewpoint of equity shareholders and the stock market: "How does the market value the company's earnings, assets and growth prospects?" or "What return or value is the company generating for its shareholders?"
  • Inputs from the market: market price per share, market capitalisation, EPS, DPS, book value per share.
  • Used to judge whether a share is under- or over-valued, the earnings per share, and dividend & growth expectations.
Earnings Per ShareEPS = (Profit After Tax − Preference Dividend) / Number of Ordinary Shares = Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders / Number of Ordinary Shares
Price-Earnings RatioP/E = Market Price Per Share / EPS
Dividend Per ShareDPS = Total Ordinary Dividends / Number of Ordinary Shares
Dividend Payout RatioPayout = DPS / EPS × 100 = Total Dividends / Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders × 100
Dividend YieldDividend Yield = DPS / Market Price Per Share × 100
Market CapitalisationMarket Cap = Market Price Per Share × Number of Shares Outstanding
Enterprise ValueEV = Market Capitalisation + Total Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents
Slide corrections

The EV slide's formula image repeats the market-cap formula; the formula above is the standard one and matches the slide's description ("money invested by shareholders and money borrowed, while subtracting the company's cash"). The DPS slide heading says "(EPS)" — it means DPS.

  • P/E — how much investors pay for each ₹1 of current earnings. High P/E → strong growth expected (or overvalued); low → low growth expected, high risk, or undervalued.
  • Market cap — not technically a ratio, but the market value of equity; analysed alongside investor ratios.
  • EV — what the entire business is worth to owners and lenders together; used in valuation, M&A and investment analysis (EV/EBITDA in Session 10).
  • Also handy: book value per share = shareholders' equity / number of shares, and P/B = market price / BVPS.

Worked example 2 — EPS with preference shares; EV with debt
  1. Kesar Dairy Ltd. (fictional): PAT ₹5,00,00,000, preference dividend ₹50,00,000, 1,00,00,000 ordinary shares, price ₹90. EPS = (5,00,00,000 − 50,00,000) / 1,00,00,000 = ₹4.50; P/E = 90 / 4.50 = 20.00x. Preference dividend belongs to preference holders, so it is removed first.
  2. Company P vs Q, both market cap ₹100 crore. P: no debt, cash ₹10 crore → EV = 100 + 0 − 10 = ₹90 crore. Q: debt ₹60 crore, cash ₹5 crore → EV = 100 + 60 − 5 = ₹155 crore. Same share price tag, but buying all of Q means taking on its debt too — it is much more expensive.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to subtract the preference dividend in EPS.
  • Dividend yield on face value (₹1.50 / ₹10 = 15% is the "dividend rate") instead of market price (2.5%).
  • Reading a negative P/E — when EPS is negative, P/E is not meaningful.
  • EV: adding cash instead of subtracting it, or leaving out short-term borrowings from debt.
Recap EPS ₹3.75, P/E 16x, DPS ₹1.50, payout 40%, yield 2.5%, market cap ₹30 crore, EV ₹34 crore. Market ratios add the share price to the accounting numbers.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Liquidity ratios check whether current assets cover current liabilities; efficiency ratios measure how many days cash sits in stock and receivables, and the operating cycle nets off the credit suppliers give. Return ratios divide profit by what was invested — EBIT by capital employed for all providers, PAT by equity for shareholders — and DuPont shows whether ROE comes from margin, asset turnover or leverage. Market ratios bring in the share price to show what investors pay for those earnings. For Tiffinwala the set tells one story: liquid and solvent, but slow working capital and thinner margins are dragging returns that leverage is propping up.

Practice & self-check

Current assets ₹12,00,000 (inventory ₹5,00,000, cash ₹1,00,000, marketable securities ₹50,000, rest receivables); current liabilities ₹6,00,000. Compute current, quick and cash ratios.

Current = 12,00,000 / 6,00,000 = 2.00. Quick = (12,00,000 − 5,00,000) / 6,00,000 = 7/6 = 1.17. Cash = (1,00,000 + 50,000) / 6,00,000 = 0.25. Meets both rules of thumb; only a quarter of dues are covered by cash.

Opening inventory ₹4,00,000, closing ₹6,00,000, COGS ₹30,00,000. Find inventory turnover and holding period.

Average inventory = (4,00,000 + 6,00,000) / 2 = ₹5,00,000. Turnover = 30,00,000 / 5,00,000 = 6.00x. Holding period = 5,00,000 / 30,00,000 × 365 = 60.8 days (60.83).

Net credit sales ₹36,50,000; opening receivables ₹4,00,000; closing ₹6,00,000. Credit terms are 30 days. Compute receivables turnover and collection period; comment.

Average receivables = ₹5,00,000. Turnover = 36,50,000 / 5,00,000 = 7.30x. Collection period = 5,00,000 / 36,50,000 × 365 = 50 days — 20 days beyond the 30-day terms; tighten collections.

Inventory days 60, receivable days 45, payable days 40. Find the operating cycle. What if the company negotiates payable days up to 55?

Operating cycle = 60 + 45 − 40 = 65 days. With 55 payable days: 60 + 45 − 55 = 50 days — 15 fewer days of cash locked up.

EBIT ₹90,00,000; PAT ₹54,00,000; total assets opening ₹5,00,00,000, closing ₹7,00,00,000; closing current liabilities ₹1,00,00,000; equity opening ₹3,00,00,000, closing ₹3,40,00,000. Compute ROCE (closing CE), ROA and ROE.

CE = 7,00,00,000 − 1,00,00,000 = ₹6,00,00,000 → ROCE = 90,00,000 / 6,00,00,000 × 100 = 15.00%.
Average TA = ₹6,00,00,000 → ROA = 54,00,000 / 6,00,00,000 × 100 = 9.00%.
Average equity = ₹3,20,00,000 → ROE = 54,00,000 / 3,20,00,000 × 100 = 16.88% (16.875).

NPM 8%, asset turnover 1.5x, equity multiplier 2.0x. Find ROE. If the firm repays debt so the multiplier falls to 1.5x, what is the new ROE?

ROE = 0.08 × 1.5 × 2.0 = 0.24 = 24%. New ROE = 0.08 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 0.18 = 18%. ROA stays 12% (0.08 × 1.5); only the leverage boost shrinks.

PAT ₹12 crore, preference dividend ₹2 crore, 5 crore ordinary shares, price ₹40, DPS ₹0.80. Find EPS, P/E, payout and dividend yield.

EPS = (12 − 2) crore / 5 crore = ₹2.00. P/E = 40 / 2 = 20x. Payout = 0.80 / 2.00 × 100 = 40%. Dividend yield = 0.80 / 40 × 100 = 2%.

Concept: a company reports a current ratio of 4.0 and ROE of 25% with D/E 3.0. Is it in great shape?

Not necessarily. A current ratio of 4 can mean cash idle or stuck in slow stock and debtors (check the quick ratio and turnover days). A 25% ROE with D/E 3.0 is mostly leverage (high equity multiplier) — check ROA and interest coverage; a dip in EBIT could wipe out equity.
