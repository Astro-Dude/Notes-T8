1 The three statements & how they link

Explain like I'm 5

The balance sheet is a photo of your wallet and EMIs on 31 March. The P&L is the video of how much you earned and spent during the year. The cash flow statement is your bank passbook, sorted into "from my job", "bought/sold stuff" and "borrowed/repaid".

StatementAlso calledShowsTimeKey line
Balance sheetStatement of financial positionWhat it owns, owes, and what belongs to ownersA point in time (31 March)Assets = Equity + Liabilities
Income statementStatement of profit and loss (P&L)Financial performance: income − expensesA period (month, quarter, FY)Profit after tax
Cash flow statementCFSInflows and outflows of cash & cash equivalents, by activityA periodOpening cash + net change = closing cash

A full Ind AS set also has a statement of changes in equity and notes (accounting policies, breakdowns) — the full-disclosure convention from Session 1.

Recap Balance sheet = position on a date; P&L and CFS = a period. PAT flows into retained earnings; the CFS explains the change in the balance-sheet cash line.

2 Balance sheet (statement of financial position)

Explain like I'm 5

List everything your chai shop owns on 31 March (kettles, stock, cash), then everything it owes (milkman, bank). Whatever is left over is yours — that's equity.

Long-term investments: strategic vs financial

  • Strategic — to support the company's business strategy (subsidiaries, joint ventures, associates).
  • Financial — mainly for a return: dividends, interest, capital appreciation.

Intangible assets

  • Identifiable, non-monetary, no physical substance, controlled, expected to give future benefits.
  • Examples: patents, copyrights, trademarks, brands, software licences, franchises, customer relationships, technology, R&D (development costs, if conditions are met). Goodwill is shown separately and arises only in mergers & acquisitions.
  • Recognise only if both: (1) future economic benefits are probable, and (2) the cost can be measured reliably.
  • Initial measurement: at cost. Purchased: e.g. a patent bought for ₹10 crore. Internally generated: only development-phase costs that meet the criteria (e.g. building in-house software); research-phase costs are expensed.
Subsequent measurement — two modelsCost model: Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated amortisation − Accumulated impairment losses
Revaluation model: Carrying amount = Fair value at revaluation − subsequent amortisation/impairment
Worked example — patent (own)

Patent bought for ₹10 crore, useful life 5 years, cost model. After 2 years an impairment of ₹1 crore is found.

Amortisation = 10 ÷ 5 = ₹2 crore/yr → accumulated after 2 years = ₹4 crore → carrying amount = 10 − 4 − 1 = ₹5 crore.

Revaluation model instead: fair value ₹7 crore at end of year 2 → carried at ₹7 crore, then amortised over the remaining 3 years ≈ ₹2.33 crore/yr (rounded).

Slide note: the slide's recognition criterion (2) repeats criterion (1), and its "internally generated" example repeats the purchased one. The versions above are the correct ones.

Equity components

  • Share capital — face (par) value of shares issued (ordinary/equity and preference).
  • Securities premium — amount received above face value.
  • Retained earnings — accumulated profits kept in the business, not paid as dividends.
Worked example — share issue at a premium (from the homework)

BVC Ltd. issues 30,000 equity shares of ₹10 face value at ₹15. Cash = 30,000 × 15 = ₹4,50,000. Share capital = 30,000 × 10 = ₹3,00,000. Securities premium = 30,000 × 5 = ₹1,50,000. A +4,50,000 = E +4,50,000.

Common mistakes
  • Putting a loan repayable in 3 years under current liabilities (only the instalment due within 12 months is current).
  • Showing securities premium as income. It is equity, not profit.
  • Listing goodwill you "built" yourself. Goodwill is recognised only when bought in an acquisition.
Recap Assets (non-current, current) = equity (share capital, premium, retained earnings) + liabilities (non-current, current). Movements in each line tie back to the P&L and CFS.

3 Depreciation — SLM & WDV

Explain like I'm 5

A ₹60,000 phone that lasts 3 years "costs" you about ₹20,000 a year, not ₹60,000 in the year you bought it. Depreciation spreads the price over the years you use it.

  • Depreciation = spreading the cost of a non-current asset (PPE) over its useful life, in which it generates economic benefits. For intangibles the same idea is called amortisation.
  • Why: the asset helps earn revenue for several periods, so its cost is expensed over those periods — the matching principle.
  • It is a non-cash expense: the cash left when the asset was bought.
TermMeaningSlide example
CostAmount initially recognised for the asset (price + installation, freight etc.)Machine ₹10,00,000
Residual valueExpected disposal amount at end of life, after disposal costs₹1,00,000 after 5 years
Useful lifePeriod (or units) the business expects to use it5 years
Depreciable amountPart of cost that will be used up = Cost − Residual value₹11,00,000 − ₹1,00,000 = ₹10,00,000
Carrying amountCost − Accumulated depreciation (book value / WDV)Closing balance each year

Straight-line method (SLM)

SLMAnnual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) / Useful life

Same amount every year → carrying amount falls in a straight line to the residual value.

Worked example — slides: machine ₹10,00,000, residual ₹1,00,000, 5 years

Annual depreciation = (10,00,000 − 1,00,000) / 5 = 9,00,000 / 5 = ₹1,80,000.

Worked example — class note: ABC Manufacturing, ₹12,00,000, residual ₹2,00,000, 5 years

Reducing balance / written-down value method (WDV)

WDVAnnual depreciation = Opening carrying amount × Depreciation rate
  • Fixed percentage of a falling balance → high charge early, lower later. Suits assets that lose value fast (vehicles, computers).
  • The balance never reaches zero, and with a given rate it rarely lands exactly on the residual value. The rate that does is:
WDV rate that reaches the residual valuer = 1 − (Residual value / Cost)1/n

Part-year depreciation

Asset bought mid-year → charge only for the months used. BVC Ltd. (homework) buys equipment on 1 October 2025 for ₹5,00,000, life 4 years, residual ₹20,000: full year = (5,00,000 − 20,000)/4 = ₹1,20,000 → FY 2025-26 (Oct–Mar, 6 months) = 1,20,000 × 6/12 = ₹60,000.

Common mistakes
  • Applying the WDV rate to the original cost every year — that's SLM, not WDV.
  • Forgetting to subtract residual value in SLM (or subtracting it in WDV).
  • Charging a full year on an asset bought in October.
  • Depreciating land (it has no finite useful life) — buildings on it are depreciated.
Recap SLM = (Cost − Residual)/Life every year. WDV = Opening balance × rate. Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation. Depreciation is a non-cash expense that applies matching.

4 Income statement (statement of profit and loss)

Explain like I'm 5

Your chai stall's year: money from selling chai, minus milk and tea, minus rent and helper's salary, minus the "wear" on your stove, minus interest on the stall loan, minus tax. What's left is your profit.

Shows financial performance over a period. Objectives (slides): determine profitability, measure operating performance, compare performance (across years and companies).

Common mistakes
  • COGS = purchases. It's purchases adjusted for opening and closing stock.
  • Putting interest above EBIT or other income inside revenue from operations.
  • Computing tax on EBIT instead of on PBT.
  • Deducting dividends to arrive at PAT.
Recap Revenue − COGS = gross profit − opex (excl. D&A) = EBITDA − D&A = EBIT + other income − finance costs = PBT − tax = PAT. EPS = PAT / shares.

5 Cash flow statement

Explain like I'm 5

Your passbook says you started the year with ₹4,000 and ended with ₹2,400. The CFS sorts every entry: salary and expenses (operating), buying/selling a scooter (investing), taking or repaying a loan (financing).

  • Shows inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents (cash on hand, bank balances, deposits maturing within 3 months) during the period.
  • The P&L shows profit; the CFS explains how the cash position changed.
ActivityInflowsOutflows
Operating — normal day-to-day businessCash from customers, for services, other operating receiptsPayments to suppliers, salaries & wages, rent & other operating expenses, taxes paid
Investing — long-term assets and investmentsSale of machinery, buildings, investments; proceeds from other long-term assets; interest and dividend received (proforma)Purchase of machinery & equipment, buildings, long-term investments
Financing — owners and lendersIssue of equity/preference shares, debentures & bonds; new bank loansBuyback of shares, dividends paid, interest paid, repayment of borrowings

Indirect method — why adjust profit?

Profit is on the accrual basis; cash isn't. Two kinds of adjustment (slides):

  1. Non-cash items: add back depreciation & amortisation (reduce profit, no cash out), stock-based compensation (an expense paid in shares, not cash); adjust deferred taxes (book tax vs cash tax). The proforma also removes non-operating items: add back finance costs (shown in financing), subtract interest/dividend income (shown in investing), subtract gains / add losses on asset sales (the full proceeds go in investing).
  2. Changes in operating working capital:
ItemIncreaseDecreaseWhy
Trade receivablesSubtractAddSales recorded but cash not yet collected / old sales collected
InventoriesSubtractAddCash spent on more stock / sold existing stock without buying
Prepaid expenses & other current assetsSubtractAddPaid in advance / using up earlier payments
Trade payablesAddSubtractBills delayed, cash stays inside / debts paid off
Accrued expenses, advances from customersAddSubtractExpense not yet paid, or cash received before revenue

Rule of thumb: current asset ↑ → cash ↓; current liability ↑ → cash ↑. (The slide's first working-capital bullet is receivables — its label is missing on the slide.)

Common mistakes
  • Adding back a gain on sale. Gains are subtracted (losses added); the proceeds go in investing.
  • Sign flips on working capital: an increase in receivables is a minus.
  • Thinking depreciation "generates" cash. It's added back only because it was deducted without any cash leaving.
  • Using tax expense instead of tax paid.
Recap CFO (indirect) = PBT + non-cash/non-operating adjustments ± working-capital changes − tax paid. Add CFI and CFF; opening cash + net change must equal balance-sheet cash.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

The balance sheet is the position on a date; the P&L and cash flow statement explain the year in between. Accrual accounting puts revenue when earned and expenses when incurred — including depreciation, which spreads capital expenditure over an asset's life by SLM or WDV. PAT flows into retained earnings, and the CFS starts from profit, strips out non-cash items, adjusts for working capital, then adds investing and financing flows to explain exactly how opening cash became closing cash. Because of those adjustments, profit and cash can move in opposite directions — which is why analysts read all three statements together (Sessions 3–4).

Practice & self-check

Machine: cost ₹6,00,000, residual ₹60,000, life 6 years, SLM. Annual depreciation and carrying amount after 4 years?

Depreciable amount = 6,00,000 − 60,000 = ₹5,40,000. Annual = 5,40,000 / 6 = ₹90,000. After 4 years: accumulated 3,60,000 → carrying = 6,00,000 − 3,60,000 = ₹2,40,000.

Computer servers cost ₹8,00,000; WDV at 15%. Depreciation for years 1–3 and carrying amount at the end of year 3?

Y1: 8,00,000 × 0.15 = 1,20,000 → 6,80,000. Y2: 6,80,000 × 0.15 = 1,02,000 → 5,78,000. Y3: 5,78,000 × 0.15 = 86,700 → ₹4,91,300. (Check: 8,00,000 × 0.853 = 4,91,300.)

Revenue ₹80,00,000; COGS ₹48,00,000; operating expenses excl. D&A ₹14,00,000; D&A ₹4,00,000; other income ₹1,00,000; finance costs ₹3,00,000; tax 25%; 5,00,000 shares. Find GP, EBITDA, EBIT, PBT, PAT, EPS.

GP = 80,00,000 − 48,00,000 = ₹32,00,000 · EBITDA = 32,00,000 − 14,00,000 = ₹18,00,000 · EBIT = 18,00,000 − 4,00,000 = ₹14,00,000 · PBT = 14,00,000 + 1,00,000 − 3,00,000 = ₹12,00,000 · Tax = ₹3,00,000 · PAT = ₹9,00,000 · EPS = 9,00,000 / 5,00,000 = ₹1.80.

PBT ₹6,00,000; depreciation ₹1,50,000; finance costs ₹50,000; receivables up ₹80,000; inventory down ₹30,000; payables down ₹20,000; tax paid ₹1,40,000. Net cash from operating activities?

Before WC: 6,00,000 + 1,50,000 + 50,000 = ₹8,00,000. WC: −80,000 + 30,000 − 20,000 = (₹70,000). Cash generated = ₹7,30,000. Less tax ₹1,40,000 → CFO = ₹5,90,000.

Equipment costing ₹5,00,000 with accumulated depreciation ₹3,20,000 is sold for ₹2,00,000. Gain/loss, and how does it appear in the CFS?

Carrying = 5,00,000 − 3,20,000 = ₹1,80,000 → gain = 2,00,000 − 1,80,000 = ₹20,000 (other income). CFS: subtract ₹20,000 in operating; show proceeds ₹2,00,000 as an investing inflow.

Revenue ₹40,00,000, gross margin 45%, operating expenses excluding D&A ₹9,00,000, D&A ₹2,00,000. Find COGS, GP, EBITDA and EBIT.

GP = 40,00,000 × 0.45 = ₹18,00,000 → COGS = ₹22,00,000. EBITDA = 18,00,000 − 9,00,000 = ₹9,00,000. EBIT = 9,00,000 − 2,00,000 = ₹7,00,000.

Which CFS section (per the slides' proforma)? (a) buyback of shares (b) interest received (c) purchase of a patent (d) income tax paid (e) new term loan (f) depreciation.

A company reports PAT of ₹12 lakh but negative operating cash flow. Give three possible reasons, and say whether it's a red flag.

(1) Receivables jumped — sales on long credit not yet collected; (2) inventory build-up; (3) suppliers paid faster (payables fell) or large tax/advance payments. Also possible: big non-cash gains inside profit. Occasionally fine for a fast-growing firm, but persistent profit with negative CFO is a classic warning sign of poor earnings quality.
