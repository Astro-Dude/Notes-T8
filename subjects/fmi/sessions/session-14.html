1 What a derivative is & why it exists

Explain like I'm 5

In September you book 10 kg of Diwali sweets at ₹600/kg for pickup in November. That booking slip has no value of its own — it's worth more if sweet prices jump, less if they fall. Its value is derived from the price of sweets.

  • A derivative is a contract whose value depends on (is derived from) the price of something else — the underlying.
  • In India the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 treats derivatives as securities; SEBI regulates exchange-traded derivatives.
  • Key features: settled on a future date; little or no money paid upfront (leverage); zero-sum — one side's gain is the other's loss; used to transfer risk.

Three uses, three kinds of participants

ParticipantGoalExample
HedgerReduce a risk they already faceA wheat farmer locks a selling price; an importer locks the USD/INR rate for a payment due in 3 months; a fund sells Nifty futures to protect its portfolio.
SpeculatorProfit from a view on price direction; takes on riskA trader buys Nifty futures expecting a rally after a repo-rate cut.
ArbitrageurLock in a riskless profit from mispricing between two marketsFutures trade above fair value → buy shares in the cash market, sell futures (§5).

All three are needed: hedgers transfer risk, speculators take it on and add liquidity, arbitrageurs keep prices in line.

Worked example — the speculator's leverage

A trader buys 1 lot of Nifty futures at 25,000 (illustrative lot size 50 units) → exposure = 25,000 × 50 = ₹12,50,000. Margin deposited ≈ 12% = ₹1,50,000 (illustrative).

  • Nifty rises 2% to 25,500: profit = 500 × 50 = ₹25,000 = 16.7% of the margin.
  • Nifty falls 2% to 24,500: loss = ₹25,000 = −16.7% of the margin.

Interpretation: a 2% market move becomes a 16.7% gain or loss on your money — leverage of 12,50,000/1,50,000 ≈ 8.3×.

Recap Derivative = contract priced off an underlying (stock, index, commodity, currency, rate). Hedgers cut risk, speculators take it, arbitrageurs remove mispricing.

2 OTC vs exchange-traded & the four types

Explain like I'm 5

OTC is a private deal with your neighbour — any terms you like, but you must trust him. Exchange-traded is buying from a big supermarket — fixed pack sizes, but the store guarantees every sale.

Swaps in one line: in an interest-rate swap a company with a floating-rate loan pays a bank a fixed rate (say 7%) and receives a floating rate (e.g. an overnight MIBOR-linked rate) on the same notional — turning its loan into a fixed-rate one. A currency swap exchanges principal and interest in two currencies.

Indian market context

  • Index futures started trading on BSE and NSE in June 2000; index and stock options followed in 2001. Today NSE's equity F&O segment is one of the world's most active derivatives markets by contracts traded.
  • SEBI regulates exchange-traded derivatives (including commodities since the Forward Markets Commission merged into SEBI in 2015). RBI regulates OTC interest-rate and currency derivatives offered by banks.
  • Every NSE trade is guaranteed by its clearing corporation, NSE Clearing Ltd.
  • Settlement: index futures/options are cash-settled; stock futures/options held to expiry are physically settled (shares delivered).
  • Lot sizes are fixed by the exchange under SEBI's minimum contract-value rules and are revised from time to time — the lot sizes on this page are illustrative; check NSE for current ones.
Recap OTC = custom + counterparty risk (forwards, swaps). Exchange = standard + clearing guarantee (futures, listed options). Options give a right; the others are obligations.

3 Forward contracts & payoffs

Explain like I'm 5

A farmer and a flour-mill owner shake hands in July: "100 quintals of wheat in October at ₹2,500." Whatever the mandi price is in October, that's the price.

  • A forward contract is an agreement made today to buy or sell an asset on a future date at a price fixed today (the forward price, K).
  • Long position = agrees to buy (gains if price rises). Short position = agrees to sell (gains if price falls).
  • Features: OTC and bilateral · fully customised (quantity, quality, date, delivery place) · no money changes hands at the start · settled only at maturity (delivery or cash difference) · hard to exit early · counterparty (default) risk.
Forward payoff at maturityLong = (ST − K) × quantity   ·   Short = (K − ST) × quantity
SymbolMeaning
STSpot (market) price of the underlying on the maturity date
KForward price agreed today (also called the delivery price)
quantityUnits covered by the contract

Worked example 2 — an importer hedges USD/INR

Chaiwala Pvt. Ltd. (fictional) must pay a supplier $50,000 in 3 months. Its bank offers a 3-month forward at ₹85.00/$ (illustrative). It goes long USD forward.

  • Row ₹87: rupee weakened; the forward saves ₹1,00,000 = (87 − 85) × 50,000.

Counterparty risk — the forward's big weakness

If wheat goes to ₹2,800, the farmer is tempted to default and sell in the mandi at ₹2,800. If it falls to ₹2,200, the mill may walk away. Nobody guarantees the deal, and there's no easy way to exit early. Futures were designed to fix exactly this.

Recap Long forward payoff = ST − K, short = K − ST, per unit. Locks a price both ways; customised, OTC, settled at maturity, exposed to default.

4 Futures: margins & mark-to-market

Explain like I'm 5

Instead of trusting each other, the farmer and the mill each leave a deposit with the mandi committee. Every evening the committee moves money from whoever lost that day to whoever gained. Nobody can run away owing a big amount.

  • A futures contract is a standardised forward traded on an exchange, with a clearing corporation in the middle and gains/losses settled every day.
  • Standardised: underlying, lot size (units per contract), expiry date (monthly cycle; usually three months — near, next, far — trade at once), tick size, settlement method.
  • Contract value = futures price × lot size. E.g. 25,000 × 50 = ₹12,50,000 (illustrative).
  • Novation: the clearing corporation becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. You never face the other trader's default.
  • Closing out: most positions are closed before expiry by taking the opposite trade (sell what you bought). Profit = (exit price − entry price) × lot size for a long.
  Buyer (long) ──── trade ────► Seller (short)
        │                            │
        └──► NSE Clearing Ltd ◄──────┘     after novation: two contracts, CCP in the middle
             (central counterparty)          collects margins, settles MTM daily, guarantees both

Margins

MarginWhat it isWhen
Initial marginDeposit to open a position, a % of contract value, set to cover a large one-day lossBefore you trade
Maintenance marginMinimum balance the account must keep (lower than initial)Checked daily
Margin call / variation marginIf balance < maintenance, deposit enough to bring it back to the initial marginNext day, or the broker squares off

On NSE, the initial margin is computed as SPAN + exposure margin and MTM is settled in cash daily; brokers ask for top-ups when your margin falls short. The textbook maintenance-margin model below is what exams use.

Interpretation: the seller's account shows the exact mirror (−10,000, +15,000, +30,000, −20,000, −20,000). On Day 3 the position was down ₹35,000 — 23% of the margin — on a 2.8% fall in Nifty.

Worked example 2 — hedging a share portfolio with Nifty futures

A fund holds ₹50,00,000 of Indian shares with β = 1.2. It fears a fall next month. Nifty futures at 25,000, lot size 50 (illustrative).

Number of futures contractsN = β × Portfolio value / (Futures price × Lot size)
  • N = 1.2 × 50,00,000 / (25,000 × 50) = 60,00,000 / 12,50,000 = 4.8 → sell 5 lots (short hedge).
  • If Nifty falls 4%: portfolio falls ≈ 1.2 × 4% = 4.8% → −₹2,40,000. Futures gain 1,000 pts × 50 × 5 = +₹2,50,000. Net ≈ +₹10,000.
  • If Nifty rises 4%: portfolio +₹2,40,000; futures −₹2,50,000; net ≈ −₹10,000.

Interpretation: the hedge locks today's value both ways (the ₹10,000 comes from rounding 4.8 up to 5). Hedging with options instead keeps the upside — Session 15.

Leverage cuts both ways
  • With 12% margin, every 1% move in the underlying is about 8.3% of your money. A 12% adverse move wipes out the whole margin.
  • Losses can exceed what you deposited — margin calls must be paid, or the broker squares off at a loss.
  • SEBI studies (2023, 2024) found roughly 9 in 10 individual F&O traders made net losses. Treat futures as a hedging tool first.
Recap Futures = standardised + exchange + clearing guarantee + daily MTM. Below maintenance margin → top up to initial margin. Total P&L = (exit − entry) × lot size.

5 Pricing futures: cost of carry & arbitrage

Explain like I'm 5

Buying rice today and storing it till Diwali costs you the interest on the money and godown rent. So rice for Diwali delivery should cost today's price plus those carrying costs — no more, no less.

Cost-of-carry (no income)F0 = S0 × erT   (continuous)   or   F0 = S0 × (1 + r)T   (discrete)
With dividendsF0 = (S0 − PV of dividends) × erT   ·   index with dividend yield q: F0 = S0 × e(r − q)T
SymbolMeaning
F0Fair (theoretical) futures price today
S0Spot price today
rRisk-free rate per year (decimal), e.g. 7% → 0.07
TTime to expiry in years (3 months = 0.25)
qDividend yield per year (for an index)
  • Cost of carry = interest cost of holding the asset (+ storage for commodities) − income it pays (dividends). Futures price = spot + net cost of carry.
  • Logic: "buy now and hold" must cost the same as "buy later via futures", or someone earns a free profit (arbitrage).
Worked example 1 — fair futures price, no dividend

Stock spot ₹1,000 · r = 7% · 3-month futures (T = 0.25).

  • Continuous: F = 1,000 × e0.07 × 0.25 = 1,000 × e0.0175 = 1,000 × 1.017654 = ₹1,017.65
  • Discrete: F = 1,000 × 1.070.25 = ₹1,017.06 · Simple interest: 1,000 × (1 + 0.07 × 0.25) = ₹1,017.50

Interpretation: the futures should trade about ₹17.65 above spot — the interest you'd pay to carry the share for 3 months. All three methods agree within ₹0.60; use the one your instructor uses.

Worked example 2 — with a dividend, and an index
  • Same stock pays a ₹20 dividend in 2 months. PV = 20 × e−0.07 × 2/12 = 20 × 0.988401 = ₹19.77. F = (1,000 − 19.77) × 1.017654 = 980.23 × 1.017654 = ₹997.54 — below spot, because the futures buyer misses the dividend.
  • Nifty at 25,000, r = 7%, dividend yield q = 1.5%, T = 0.25: F = 25,000 × e(0.07 − 0.015) × 0.25 = 25,000 × e0.01375 = 25,346.12 (vs 25,441.35 ignoring dividends).

Basis and convergence

  • Basis = Spot price − Futures price.
  • Contango: futures > spot (negative basis) — normal when carry cost > income. Backwardation: futures < spot (positive basis) — big dividends or scarce commodity.
  • Convergence: as expiry nears, the carry period shrinks, so the basis → 0. On expiry day futures = spot.

Common mistakes
  • Using T in months: 3 months is T = 0.25, not 3.
  • Forgetting to subtract the PV of dividends (or using the dividend's face value, not its PV).
  • Getting basis backwards. Basis = spot − futures.
  • Calling it arbitrage when the "profit" is smaller than brokerage, STT and other costs. Real arbitrage must survive costs.
Recap F = S·erT (less PV of dividends, or S·e(r−q)T). Futures above fair → cash-and-carry; below → reverse. Basis shrinks to zero at expiry.

6 Options: a first look

Explain like I'm 5

You pay a flat owner ₹20,000 as a token to hold a flat at ₹50 lakh for 3 months. If prices rise, you buy at ₹50 lakh; if they crash, you walk away and lose only the token. That token is an option premium.

  • An option gives the buyer (holder) the right but not the obligation to buy or sell the underlying at a fixed price on/before a date. The seller (writer) has the obligation if the buyer exercises.
  • Call option = right to buy. Put option = right to sell.
  • Premium = price the buyer pays the seller upfront. Strike price (K) = the fixed buy/sell price. Expiry = last date of the right.
  • European options can be exercised only on expiry; American any time up to expiry (details in Session 15).

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from an underlying; hedgers use it to shed risk, speculators to take risk with leverage, arbitrageurs to keep prices honest. A forward locks a price privately but carries default risk; a futures contract standardises it, puts a clearing corporation in the middle and settles gains and losses daily through margins. Cost of carry pins the fair futures price at spot plus interest minus income, and arbitrage pulls the market price back whenever it strays; at expiry futures and spot converge. Options change the deal from an obligation to a right, for a premium — the subject of the next two sessions.

Practice & self-check

You enter a long forward on 500 units at K = ₹1,200. At maturity the spot is ₹1,130. What are your payoff and your counterparty's?

Long: (1,130 − 1,200) × 500 = −₹35,000. Short: +₹35,000. Zero-sum.

You short 1 lot (100 units) of futures at ₹2,000. Initial margin ₹30,000, maintenance ₹22,000. Settlement prices: Day 1 ₹2,040, Day 2 ₹2,090. Show the margin account.

Short loses when price rises. Day 1: −40 × 100 = −₹4,000 → ₹26,000 (above ₹22,000, no call). Day 2: −50 × 100 = −₹5,000 → ₹21,000 < ₹22,000 → margin call ₹9,000 to restore ₹30,000.

Spot ₹800, risk-free rate 8%, no dividends. Fair price of a 6-month futures?

F = 800 × e0.08 × 0.5 = 800 × e0.04 = 800 × 1.040811 = ₹832.65. Carry cost ≈ ₹32.65.

Spot ₹500, r = 6%, 3-month futures trading at ₹520. Is there an arbitrage? How much per share?

Fair F = 500 × e0.015 = ₹507.56. Market ₹520 > fair → cash-and-carry: borrow ₹500, buy the share, sell futures at ₹520. At expiry receive ₹520, repay ₹507.56 → profit ₹12.44 per share (before costs).

A ₹1 crore portfolio has β = 0.8. Nifty futures are at 24,000 with lot size 50 (illustrative). How many lots to hedge, and which side?

N = 0.8 × 1,00,00,000 / (24,000 × 50) = 80,00,000 / 12,00,000 = 6.67 → sell about 7 lots (short hedge, because you own the shares).

Call: K = ₹300, premium ₹12. Find payoff and profit at ST = ₹330 and ₹290, and the breakeven.

At ₹330: payoff 30, profit 30 − 12 = ₹18. At ₹290: payoff 0, loss = premium ₹12. Breakeven = 300 + 12 = ₹312.

Why does a futures contract carry almost no counterparty risk while a forward does?

The clearing corporation becomes the counterparty to both sides (novation), collects initial margin, and settles losses daily via MTM. No one can build up a large unpaid loss; anyone who misses a margin call is squared off.

What is basis, and what happens to it as a futures contract approaches expiry?

Basis = spot − futures. The carry period shrinks to zero, so the basis converges to 0 and futures = spot on expiry (otherwise there would be a riskless arbitrage on the last day).
