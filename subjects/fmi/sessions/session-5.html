1 What management accounting does

Explain like I'm 5

The kirana owner's tax return (financial accounting) tells the government last year's profit. His own notebook — "should I stock the new biscuit brand, what should I charge, did I beat last month's budget?" — is management accounting.

  • Financial accounting deals with financial information and follows GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles; in India, Ind AS + Companies Act).
  • Management (managerial) accounting = the process of identifying, measuring, analysing and communicating financial and non-financial information to managers for decision-making.
  • A key component of management accounting is cost accounting.

Decision-makingChoosing the best available alternativeMake or buy · outsource vs in-house · product selection · pricing

Worked example — cost-plus price (slide's ₹80 product)

Full cost ₹80/unit. Mark-up 25% → 80 × 1.25 = ₹100. Mark-up 50% → 80 × 1.50 = ₹120.

At ₹100, profit = ₹20: that is 20/80 = 25% mark-up on cost but 20/100 = 20% margin on sales.

Common mistake

Mixing mark-up (profit ÷ cost) and margin (profit ÷ price). "20% profit on sales" on a cost of ₹80 is 80 ÷ 0.80 = ₹100, not 80 × 1.20 = ₹96.

Recap Management accounting = internal, future-focused, no fixed format; it supports planning (budgets), control (variances) and decisions (make/buy, pricing). Cost accounting is its core.

2 Cost, expense, cost object & cost unit

Explain like I'm 5

The ₹60,000 laptop is a cost the day you buy it. It becomes an expense bit by bit as you use it up over its life.

  • Cost — a sacrifice of resources, an amount paid, to achieve a specific objective (acquire an asset, make a product, provide a service).
  • Expense — a cost charged against revenue in a particular accounting period.
  • So: Cost ≠ Expense (not every cost is an expense yet) but Expense = Cost (every expense is a cost that has been used up).
  • Actual cost — already incurred. Budgeted cost — estimated in advance.
  • Cost object — anything whose resource use we want to measure: product, service, project, customer, activity, department, distribution channel.
  • Costing — the process of measuring the cost of cost objects.
Worked example — cost vs expense
  • 1 April: buy a machine for ₹12,00,000, life 5 years, no scrap → a cost recorded as an asset. Expense this year = depreciation 12,00,000 ÷ 5 = ₹2,40,000.
  • Buy raw material ₹5,00,000; goods using ₹3,50,000 of it are sold → ₹3,50,000 becomes an expense (in cost of goods sold); ₹1,50,000 is still a cost sitting in inventory (asset).

Cost per unit and cost units

Cost per unitCost per unit = Total cost ÷ Number of units = ₹50,00,000 ÷ 10,000 = ₹500

A cost unit is the unit of product or service in which cost is ascertained, accumulated and expressed.

BusinessCost unit
Automobile manufacturerPer car
HotelPer room-night
AirlinePer passenger-kilometre
Electricity companyPer kilowatt-hour (kWh)
HospitalPer patient-day
Transport companyPer tonne-kilometre
Cloud / SaaS company (extra)Per GB-month stored, per 1,000 API calls

Cost object vs cost unit: cost object = what you want costed (the Bengaluru–Mumbai route); cost unit = the measure you express it in (₹ per passenger-km).

Recap Cost = resources sacrificed; expense = the part of cost matched against this period's revenue. Cost object = thing being costed; cost unit = the per-unit measure.

3 Classifying costs

Explain like I'm 5

The same ₹100 note goes into different wallets depending on the question: what was it spent on? which department? can I pin it on one product? does it grow with output? does it matter for this decision?

Cost classification = grouping costs into logical categories by a characteristic or purpose. One cost can be classified in several ways. Slide example — steel used to make a car:

Common mistake

Direct ≠ variable, indirect ≠ fixed. Direct/indirect is about traceability; fixed/variable is about behaviour. Lubricants and factory power are indirect and variable; a one-time design fee for a single model is direct and fixed.

Recap Classify by nature (material/labour/expenses), function (production/admin/selling/distribution), traceability (direct/overhead), behaviour (fixed/variable/semi-variable) and relevance. Total cost is the same; only the grouping changes.

4 Cost behaviour & the high-low method

Explain like I'm 5

The samosa stall's rent is the same whether it sells 10 or 1,000 samosas (fixed). Potatoes grow with every samosa (variable). The electricity bill has a fixed meter charge plus units used (semi-variable).

Cost behaviour = how a cost changes when the level of activity changes.

Fixed cost — fixed in total, falls per unit

Fixed cost stays constant in total within a relevant range (existing capacity), whatever the activity: factory rent, manager's salary, insurance. Slide example: car factory rent ₹10,00,000 a month.

Fixed cost per unitFixed cost per unit = Total fixed cost ÷ Number of units
Cars producedTotal fixed cost (₹)Fixed cost per car (₹)Working

5,00010,00,00020010,00,000 ÷ 5,000
10,00010,00,00010010,00,000 ÷ 10,000

Every extra car spreads the same rent thinner — this is why volume lowers unit cost (economies of scale).

Variable cost — variable in total, constant per unit

Variable cost changes in total in proportion to activity: direct materials, direct labour paid per unit, sales commission, delivery cost per unit, power used in production. Slide example: raw material ₹20 per bottle.

Semi-variable (mixed) cost

A semi-variable cost has a fixed component (incurred even at zero activity) and a variable component (rises with activity).

Semi-variable costSemi-variable cost = Fixed component + (Variable rate × Activity)

High-low method — splitting a mixed cost

Use the highest- and lowest-activity periods; the change in cost between them must be variable cost.

High-low methodb = (Costhigh − Costlow) ÷ (Activityhigh − Activitylow)  ·  a = Costhigh − b × Activityhigh  ·  Total cost y = a + b·x
SymbolMeaning
bVariable cost per unit of activity (slope)
aFixed cost per period (intercept)
xActivity level (units, machine hours, km…)
yTotal (semi-variable) cost at activity x
Worked example — Kaveri Packaging (fictional) machine maintenance
MonthMachine hoursMaintenance cost (₹)
Apr4,0001,10,000
May6,5001,45,000
Jun5,0001,26,000
Jul (high activity)8,0001,70,000
Aug (low activity)3,00098,000
Sep (one-off repair)7,0001,72,000
  1. Pick by activity: high = Jul (8,000 h, ₹1,70,000); low = Aug (3,000 h, ₹98,000). Not Sep, even though it has the highest cost.
  2. Variable rate b = (1,70,000 − 98,000) ÷ (8,000 − 3,000) = 72,000 ÷ 5,000 = ₹14.40 per machine hour.
  3. Fixed cost a = 1,70,000 − 14.40 × 8,000 = 1,70,000 − 1,15,200 = ₹54,800 per month. Check with low: 98,000 − 14.40 × 3,000 = 98,000 − 43,200 = ₹54,800 ✓.
  4. Forecast for 9,000 hours: 54,800 + 14.40 × 9,000 = 54,800 + 1,29,600 = ₹1,84,400.

Trap: using Sep (highest cost) with Aug gives b = 74,000 ÷ 4,000 = ₹18.50 and a = ₹42,500 — wrong, because Sep's repair is not normal behaviour.

Check on the slide's electricity table: (1,30,000 − 58,000) ÷ (10,000 − 1,000) = ₹8/hour; fixed = 1,30,000 − 80,000 = ₹50,000 — exactly the slide's split.

Excel — maintenance sheet (B = hours, C = cost, data in rows 2–7)
     A                  B          C
1   Month              Hours      Cost
2   Apr                4000       110000
...
7   Sep                7000       172000
9   High hours         =MAX(B2:B7)                          → 8000
10  Low hours          =MIN(B2:B7)                          → 3000
11  Cost at high       =INDEX(C2:C7,MATCH(B9,B2:B7,0))      → 170000   not =MAX(C2:C7), which picks Sep

High-low uses only 2 of the 6 points; =SLOPE/=INTERCEPT (least squares) use all six, so Sep's repair pulls the slope up to ₹15.74 (rounded). Exams ask for high-low unless they say "regression".

Common mistakes
  • Choosing the high/low cost months instead of the high/low activity months.
  • Treating fixed cost per unit as constant: "fixed cost is ₹200/unit at 5,000 cars, so 10,000 cars cost ₹20 lakh fixed" — no, total fixed stays ₹10 lakh (₹100/unit).
  • Forgetting the relevant range: double the output beyond capacity and "fixed" rent steps up (new factory).
Recap Fixed: constant total, falling per unit. Variable: rising total, constant per unit. Semi-variable = fixed + variable; split it with high-low on the highest/lowest activity: b = Δcost ÷ Δactivity, a = cost − b·x.

5 Product cost & overhead absorption

Explain like I'm 5

A tiffin service pays ₹30,000 kitchen rent a month and packs 3,000 tiffins, so each tiffin "carries" ₹10 of rent. Spreading shared costs over units like that is overhead absorption.

Why we need cost per unit (slide)

To value inventory, set selling prices, measure and compare product profitability, control costs, and decide on production vs outsourcing. If a unit costs ₹80, management must know that before choosing a price of ₹100 or ₹120.

Components of product cost

  • Direct materials — traceable to the product (aluminium in a car).
  • Direct labour — workers who make the product (wages of the employee assembling the machine).
  • Production overheads — production costs not traceable to the product: factory rent, electricity, insurance, supervisor's salary, maintenance, security.
Total production costTotal Production Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Production Overheads
Worked example — electric motors (slide), with overheads added

Slide: direct material ₹4,000 + direct labour ₹2,000 = direct cost ₹6,000 per motor. The factory also has rent, electricity, machine depreciation, supervisor salary, maintenance, security. How much of these goes into each motor?

Extension (illustrative): budgeted production overheads ₹36,00,000 a year; budgeted output 1,200 motors.

Per motor₹Working
Direct material4,000given
Direct labour2,000given
Prime (direct) cost6,0004,000 + 2,000
Production overhead absorbed3,00036,00,000 ÷ 1,200
Total production cost9,0006,000 + 3,000

Pricing on direct cost alone (say ₹7,000) would look profitable but lose ₹2,000 a motor once overheads are counted. With a 20% mark-up on full cost: 9,000 × 1.20 = ₹10,800.

Overhead absorption rate (OAR)

Overhead absorption = charging a share of total production overheads to individual units using an appropriate absorption rate — i.e. spreading overheads across the products that benefit from them.

Overhead absorption rateOAR = Budgeted Overhead ÷ Budgeted Activity

Slide example: total factory overhead 1,00,000; units produced 20,000; units are an appropriate basis → overhead per unit = 1,00,000 ÷ 20,000 = 5 per unit (the slide uses $; arithmetic identical in ₹).

Choosing the basis matters

Worked example — Deccan Engineering (fictional): machine hours vs labour hours

Budgeted production overheads ₹20,00,000. Budget: 2,000 Motors (3 machine h + 2 labour h each) and 4,000 Pumps (1 machine h + 4 labour h each) → 10,000 machine hours, 20,000 labour hours.

BasisOAROverhead per Motor (₹)Overhead per Pump (₹)Total absorbed (₹)
Machine hours20,00,000 ÷ 10,000 = ₹200/h3 × 200 = 6001 × 200 = 20020,00,000
Labour hours20,00,000 ÷ 20,000 = ₹100/h2 × 100 = 2004 × 100 = 40020,00,000

Both recover ₹20 lakh in total, but the unit costs swing by ₹400. If the overheads are mostly machine depreciation and power, use machine hours — otherwise Pumps are over-costed and Motors under-priced.

Under- and over-absorption

OAR is set from budgets at the start of the year. Absorbed overhead = OAR × actual activity, so it rarely equals actual overhead.

Worked example — slide's OAR of ₹5 per unit

Actual production 22,000 units; actual overheads ₹1,04,000.

Absorbed = 5 × 22,000 = ₹1,10,000 > actual ₹1,04,000 → over-absorbed by ₹6,000. Product costs were overstated by ₹6,000, so ₹6,000 is added back to profit. (If absorbed < actual → under-absorbed → deducted.) This is the "over/(under)-absorbed OH" line in the absorption-costing profit statement in Session 6.

Recap Product cost = DM + DL + production overheads. Overheads enter units through OAR = budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted activity, on the basis that drives them. Absorbed − actual = over (+) or under (−) absorption.

6 The cost sheet, line by line

Explain like I'm 5

A cost sheet builds a product's price in layers, like a thali: rice and dal (materials + labour), then the kitchen's gas and rent, then the restaurant's office and delivery, then the owner's profit — the menu price.

A cost sheet is a statement showing the total cost and cost per unit of output for a period, built up stage by stage:

  Direct material consumed + Direct labour + Direct expenses   = PRIME COST
+ Factory (works) overheads                                    = Gross works cost
+ Opening WIP − Closing WIP                                    = WORKS (FACTORY) COST
+ Quality control / production-related admin − Scrap value     = COST OF PRODUCTION
+ Opening finished goods − Closing finished goods              = COST OF GOODS SOLD
+ Administration overheads + Selling & distribution overheads = COST OF SALES (total cost)
+ Profit                                                       = SALES

Worked example — quoting a price from the cost sheet

A dealer wants a quote. Cost of sales ₹396/chair; Shakti wants 20% profit on sales.

Price = Cost ÷ (1 − margin) = 396 ÷ 0.80 = ₹495. Check: profit 99 ÷ 495 = 20% ✓. The same ₹495 is a 25% mark-up on cost (396 × 1.25).

Common mistakes in cost sheets
  • Using purchases instead of material consumed (forgetting opening/closing raw-material stock).
  • Carriage inwards → material cost; carriage outwards → distribution overhead.
  • Adjusting WIP before adding factory overheads, or valuing finished stock at prime cost.
  • Putting selling, distribution or general admin costs into cost of production (they are period costs).
  • Adding scrap value instead of deducting it.
Recap Prime cost → + factory OH ± WIP = works cost → + QC − scrap = cost of production → ± finished stock = COGS → + admin + S&D = cost of sales → + profit = sales.

7 Relevant, marginal & opportunity costs

Explain like I'm 5

You paid ₹500 for a movie ticket and the movie is terrible. The ₹500 is gone whether you stay or leave (sunk) — decide only on what happens next. If staying means missing a ₹1,000 freelance gig, that ₹1,000 is the opportunity cost of staying.

Classification by relevance

CostDefinition (slide)Use in a decision?
Relevant costA future cost that changes between alternativesYes
Irrelevant costA cost that does not change between alternativesNo
Sunk costAlready incurred and cannot be recoveredNo — never
Opportunity costBenefit sacrificed by choosing one alternative over another = the best alternative forgoneYes — add it to the option that gives up the benefit
Differential (incremental) costDifference in total cost between two optionsYes — the core of the comparison
Committed / unavoidable fixed costIncurred whichever option is chosenNo
Worked example — sunk cost (slide) with numbers added

A tech company spent ₹2 crore building a software platform; a competitor then launched a superior product. Option A: launch. Option B: abandon. The ₹2 crore is sunk and should not decide anything.

Extension (illustrative): launching needs ₹80 lakh more marketing and brings ₹1.5 crore contribution; abandoning lets it sell the code for ₹30 lakh.

Decision: launch — better by ₹40 lakh either way. Subtracting a sunk cost from both options never changes the ranking; it only tempts managers to "throw good money after bad" or abandon a good option.

Worked example — opportunity cost (slide)

A student can take a job at ₹16 lakh a year or do a full-time MBA. Choosing the MBA → opportunity cost = ₹16 lakh salary forgone (plus fees, the out-of-pocket cost). Choosing the job → opportunity cost = the better managerial jobs the MBA would have opened.

Worked example — relevant cost of material already in stock

A job needs 500 kg of material X. The store holds 500 kg bought last year at ₹200/kg (₹1,00,000). Today's replacement price ₹260/kg; scrap value ₹80/kg.

  • The ₹1,00,000 paid last year is sunk — ignore.
  • If X is used regularly, using it now means buying more later → relevant cost = replacement 500 × 260 = ₹1,30,000.
  • If X has no other use, the only thing given up is the scrap sale → relevant cost = 500 × 80 = ₹40,000.

Marginal cost

Marginal cost = the additional cost of producing one more unit. In practice it equals the variable cost per unit, provided fixed costs do not change.

Marginal costMarginal Cost = Change in Total Cost ÷ Change in Output

Slide: 10,000 components cost ₹50,00,000; 10,100 cost ₹50,60,000. MC = (50,60,000 − 50,00,000) ÷ (10,100 − 10,000) = 60,000 ÷ 100 = ₹600 per additional component.

Marginal costing and contribution

Marginal costing is a technique: variable costs are charged to products and fixed costs are treated as period costs. Its central idea is contribution. Note: Marginal Costing ≠ Marginal Cost (a technique vs a per-unit cost figure).

ContributionContribution = Sales − Variable Cost  ·  Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit = Selling Price − Marginal Cost

Full CVP and break-even analysis using contribution → Session 6.

No capacity constraint → price above marginal cost is a gain

Worked example — the last empty seat (slide)

Normal fare ₹8,000. Aircraft lease, crew, airport charges are already committed. Extra cost of one more passenger: meal 500 + passenger service 200 + processing 100 + other 200 = MC ₹1,000. A customer offers ₹3,000.

Additional contribution = 3,000 − 1,000 = ₹2,000 → sell, since the seat would otherwise fly empty and earn nothing.

Capacity constraint → add the opportunity cost

A capacity constraint exists when Demand (required capacity) > Available capacity. The scarce resource is the limiting factor or bottleneck.

Worked example — Bengaluru–Mumbai flight (slide, all six parts)

180 seats; 175 sold at average ₹8,000; a travel company wants 10 seats at ₹3,000; individual passengers would pay ₹7,000 for the remaining seats; MC as above.

  1. Capacity: 180 − 175 = 5 seats available; demand 10 → constraint = 5 seats.
  2. Marginal cost per passenger = 500 + 200 + 100 + 200 = ₹1,000.
  3. Contribution from the travel company = 3,000 − 1,000 = ₹2,000 per seat → 5 × 2,000 = ₹10,000 for the 5 seats.
  4. Contribution from an individual = 7,000 − 1,000 = ₹6,000 per seat → 5 × 6,000 = ₹30,000.
  5. Opportunity cost of giving a seat to the travel company = contribution forgone = ₹6,000 per seat.
  6. Minimum acceptable price = MC + opportunity cost = 1,000 + 6,000 = ₹7,000.

Decision: reject ₹3,000. Accepting earns 5 × (6,000 − 2,000) = ₹20,000 less contribution (₹30,000 − ₹10,000) than selling to individuals.

Slide correction: part 3 on the slide reads "₹6,000 – ₹1,000 = ₹7,000"; it should be ₹7,000 − ₹1,000 = ₹6,000 (the later parts correctly use ₹6,000).

Key takeaway

Minimum price = marginal cost when there is spare capacity; = marginal cost + opportunity cost (contribution forgone) when capacity is full. Sunk and committed costs never enter.

Common mistakes
  • Using full cost (including committed fixed costs like the aircraft lease) for a one-off decision — you would wrongly reject the ₹3,000 empty-seat offer.
  • Ignoring opportunity cost when capacity is full — you would wrongly accept it.
  • Counting past spend ("we've already invested ₹2 crore") as a reason to continue.
Recap Relevant = future + different between options. Ignore sunk and committed costs; include opportunity costs. MC = Δcost ÷ Δoutput ≈ variable cost/unit; contribution = price − MC.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Management accounting turns costs into decisions. First classify every cost — by element, function, traceability, behaviour and relevance — because each manager's question needs a different grouping. Behaviour (fixed vs variable, split with high-low) tells you how cost moves with volume; absorption and the cost sheet give the full cost per unit for inventory and normal pricing. For one-off decisions, drop sunk and committed costs and compare only relevant ones: marginal cost plus any opportunity cost sets the minimum price. Session 6 builds break-even and CVP analysis on this fixed/variable split.

Practice & self-check

Opening raw material ₹50,000, purchases ₹3,00,000, closing raw material ₹70,000, direct wages ₹1,20,000, direct expenses ₹30,000, factory overheads ₹90,000, opening WIP ₹20,000, closing WIP ₹30,000, admin overheads ₹40,000, S&D overheads ₹60,000, no finished stock, sales ₹7,50,000. Find prime cost, works cost, cost of sales and profit.

Material consumed = 50,000 + 3,00,000 − 70,000 = ₹2,80,000. Prime cost = 2,80,000 + 1,20,000 + 30,000 = ₹4,30,000. Gross works = 4,30,000 + 90,000 = ₹5,20,000; works cost = 5,20,000 + 20,000 − 30,000 = ₹5,10,000 (= cost of production = COGS, no QC/scrap/finished stock). Cost of sales = 5,10,000 + 40,000 + 60,000 = ₹6,10,000. Profit = 7,50,000 − 6,10,000 = ₹1,40,000 (18.67% of sales).

Units produced and total cost over four months: 2,000 → ₹46,000; 3,500 → ₹66,000; 5,000 → ₹85,000; 4,200 → ₹74,000. Use high-low to find variable cost per unit, fixed cost, and cost at 4,000 units.

High 5,000/₹85,000; low 2,000/₹46,000. b = (85,000 − 46,000) ÷ (5,000 − 2,000) = 39,000 ÷ 3,000 = ₹13/unit. a = 46,000 − 13 × 2,000 = ₹20,000 (check: 85,000 − 65,000 = 20,000). Cost at 4,000 = 20,000 + 52,000 = ₹72,000.

Budgeted overheads ₹9,00,000; budgeted machine hours 15,000. Actual hours 16,000; actual overheads ₹9,30,000. Find OAR and the under/over-absorption.

OAR = 9,00,000 ÷ 15,000 = ₹60/machine hour. Absorbed = 60 × 16,000 = ₹9,60,000 vs actual ₹9,30,000 → over-absorbed ₹30,000 (added back to profit).

Rent is ₹6,00,000 a month. Output rises from 3,000 to 4,000 units. What happens to total rent, rent per unit, and material cost per unit (₹45)?

Total rent unchanged at ₹6,00,000 (fixed, within the relevant range). Rent per unit falls from 6,00,000 ÷ 3,000 = ₹200 to 6,00,000 ÷ 4,000 = ₹150. Material per unit stays ₹45 (variable); total material rises from ₹1,35,000 to ₹1,80,000.

A 40-seat bus has 4 empty seats. Regular fare ₹1,200; marginal cost per passenger ₹150. A group wants 6 seats at ₹700 each; 2 regular passengers would have to be turned away. Accept? What is the minimum price for those 2 seats?

Contribution per group seat = 700 − 150 = ₹550; per regular seat = 1,200 − 150 = ₹1,050. Accept all 6: 6 × 550 − 2 × 1,050 (opportunity cost) = 3,300 − 2,100 = +₹1,200 → better than rejecting. Minimum price for the 2 displaced seats = MC + opportunity cost = 150 + 1,050 = ₹1,200; for the 4 empty seats it is ₹150. If the group would take just 4 seats, that is best of all: 4 × 550 + 2 × 1,050 = ₹4,300.

Classify by behaviour and traceability: (a) cloth for shirts, (b) factory insurance, (c) 2% sales commission, (d) phone bill = fixed rental + per-call charges, (e) supervisor's salary.

(a) Variable, direct material. (b) Fixed, indirect (production overhead). (c) Variable, selling overhead. (d) Semi-variable, indirect. (e) Fixed (step-fixed if more shifts are added), indirect labour.

A firm spent ₹5 lakh on market research for a product it is now deciding whether to launch. Is the ₹5 lakh relevant? What would be relevant?

No — it is a sunk cost, spent whatever the decision. Relevant: future incremental revenues and costs of launching (production, marketing, extra working capital) and any opportunity cost (e.g. the capacity could make another product).

Give three differences between financial accounting and management accounting.

(1) Users: external (investors, lenders, regulators) vs internal managers. (2) Rules: GAAP/Ind AS and Companies Act mandatory vs no fixed format. (3) Focus: past results vs future planning and decisions. Also: whole company vs segments; monetary only vs financial + non-financial; periodic vs whenever needed.
