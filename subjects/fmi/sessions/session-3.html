1 Ratio analysis — what, why & the five types

Explain like I'm 5

A chai stall that made ₹500 profit today: good or bad? If sales were ₹2,000 that is 25 paise per rupee; if sales were ₹20,000 it is 2.5 paise. A ratio puts one number next to another so it means something.

  • Financial ratios = key metrics, derived from the financial statements, used to judge a company's financial health and performance.
  • They let you compare a company with itself over time and with industry peers, regardless of size.

Objectives (as in the class slides)

  1. Profitability — does the business earn enough profit for owners and other stakeholders?
  2. Liquidity — can it meet its short-term obligations?
  3. Operational efficiency — does it use its assets efficiently?
  4. Solvency & financial risk — can it meet its long-term obligations?
  5. Returns on capital employed — what do owners and lenders earn on what they provided?
  6. Comparisons — across time, competitors, industry and budgeted figures.
  7. Managerial decision-making support.

3 Profitability (margin) ratios

Explain like I'm 5

Out of every ₹100 a tiffin sells for, how many rupees are left after paying for the food, then the staff, then the bank, then the tax? Each "left over" is a margin.

  • Slides: profitability & return ratios measure a company's ability to generate profit from its sales, assets, capital employed and shareholders' funds.
  • Two groups: profit margin ratios = profit ÷ revenue (this section) and return ratios = profit ÷ capital, assets or equity (ROCE, ROA, ROE — Session 4).
  • Every margin uses the same denominator (revenue); only the profit level in the numerator changes.

Gross Profit Margin (GPM)

Percentage of revenue left after deducting cost of goods sold / cost of sales. Judges the basic production or purchasing activity, before operating expenses, finance costs and taxes.

Gross Profit MarginGPM = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100, where Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Sales
Same thing, one lineGPM = (Revenue − Cost of Sales) / Revenue × 100

EBITDA margin (not on the class slide; widely used)

EBITDA marginEBITDA margin = EBITDA / Revenue × 100, where EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation & Amortisation

Strips out the non-cash depreciation charge, so firms with old vs new assets (or SLM vs WDV) compare more fairly.

Operating Profit Margin (OPM)

Uses EBIT (earnings before interest & tax): profit after operating expenses and depreciation/amortisation, but before interest and tax.

Operating Profit MarginOPM = EBIT / Revenue × 100

Net Profit Margin (NPM)

Percentage of revenue that remains as profit after all relevant expenses: operating expenses, finance costs and taxation.

Net Profit MarginNPM = Profit After Tax / Revenue × 100

Worked example 2 — high sales, low margin (Sabzi Mandi Retail Ltd., fictional grocery chain)

Revenue ₹1,00,00,00,000 · COGS ₹85,00,00,000 · operating expenses ₹10,00,00,000 · D&A ₹1,00,00,000 · interest ₹50,00,000 · tax 25%.

  1. Gross profit = 1,00,00,00,000 − 85,00,00,000 = ₹15,00,00,000 → GPM = 15/100 × 100 = 15.00%
  2. EBITDA = 15,00,00,000 − 10,00,00,000 = ₹5,00,00,000 → EBITDA margin = 5.00%
  3. EBIT = 5,00,00,000 − 1,00,00,000 = ₹4,00,00,000 → OPM = 4.00%
  4. PBT = 4,00,00,000 − 50,00,000 = ₹3,50,00,000; tax = ₹87,50,000; PAT = ₹2,62,50,000 → NPM = 2.63% (2.625%)

Interpretation: Sabzi Mandi earns more rupees of profit than Tiffinwala (₹2.63 crore vs ₹1.88 crore) on a margin 3.6 times thinner. Margins measure efficiency per rupee of sales, not size — a grocer lives on volume, a food brand on margin. Compare margins only within the same industry.

Common mistakes
  • Using PBT instead of PAT for net margin, or EBITDA instead of EBIT for operating margin — the slide definitions are PAT and EBIT.
  • Confusing margin (÷ price) with markup (÷ cost).
  • Putting interest above EBIT. Interest is a financing cost: it comes after EBIT.
  • Including one-off gains (sale of land, other income) in operating profit — they inflate OPM without any operating improvement.
  • Comparing a % with a decimal: 0.0938 and 9.38% are the same number.
Recap GPM = (Revenue − COGS)/Revenue; OPM = EBIT/Revenue; NPM = PAT/Revenue (all × 100). Same denominator, deeper profit line. A common-size P&L shows which cost line ate the margin.

4 Leverage (solvency) ratios

Explain like I'm 5

You buy a ₹1,00,000 phone with ₹30,000 of your own money and a ₹70,000 EMI loan. Your "debt-to-equity" is 70/30 = 2.33. The bigger the loan share, the scarier a salary cut — the EMI still has to be paid.

  • Solvency ratios (also called leverage ratios) measure a company's ability to meet its long-term financial obligations.
  • The slide question: "Does the company have a financially sustainable level of debt, and can it meet its long-term interest and principal repayment obligations?"
  • Liquidity ratios look at the next 12 months; solvency ratios look at long-term stability and capital structure (the mix of debt and equity).

Debt-to-Equity (D/E) ratio

Compares debt financing with shareholders' equity: how much the company relies on borrowed funds relative to owners' funds.

Debt-to-EquityD/E = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity
TermSlide definitionTiffinwala 31 Mar 2026
DebtTotal interest-bearing debt or total liabilitiesInterest-bearing: 4,00,00,000 + 1,00,00,000 = ₹5,00,00,000 · Total liabilities: 5,00,00,000 + 1,50,00,000 + 87,50,000 = ₹7,37,50,000
EquityShare capital + Reserves & Surplus (retained earnings)5,00,00,000 + 5,12,50,000 = ₹10,12,50,000

Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)

Measures the company's ability to pay its interest expense out of operating profit: "How many times can operating profit cover the interest expense?"

Interest Coverage RatioICR = EBIT / Interest Expense

Liquidity vs solvency (don't mix them up)

LiquiditySolvency / leverage
HorizonShort term (within 12 months)Long term
QuestionCan it pay this year's bills?Is its debt level sustainable; can it pay interest + principal over the years?
UsesCurrent assets vs current liabilitiesDebt vs equity; EBIT vs interest
RatiosCurrent, quick, cashD/E, interest coverage, debt ratio, DSCR
A firm can be…solvent but illiquid (profitable, but cash stuck in receivables) or liquid but insolvent (cash today, crushing long-term debt).
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting short-term borrowings (cash credit, current maturities of long-term debt) when adding up debt.
  • Mixing the two D/E definitions — say "interest-bearing debt" or "total liabilities" in your answer.
  • Using PAT or EBITDA in interest coverage — the slide formula uses EBIT.
  • Using market capitalisation as "equity" — leverage ratios use book equity (share capital + reserves & surplus).
  • Reading D/E alone: 0.49 is safe only if EBIT covers interest; a firm with low D/E but collapsing EBIT is still at risk.
Recap D/E = Total debt / Shareholders' equity; ICR = EBIT / Interest. Tiffinwala: D/E 0.49, ICR 6.00x — moderate, comfortable debt. Leverage raises ROE in good years and deepens losses in bad ones.

Limitations of ratio analysis

  • Historical — built on past statements; the future may differ.
  • Accounting choices — SLM vs WDV depreciation, inventory valuation, revenue recognition change the ratios of identical businesses.
  • Year-end snapshot — balance-sheet figures are one day's numbers; seasonal businesses look different in March vs October. Using averages helps.
  • Window dressing — e.g. repaying a loan on 30 March and re-borrowing on 2 April flatters D/E.
  • Inflation & size — old assets at historical cost understate capital; ratios ignore absolute size (§3 example 2).
  • Different definitions — "debt" or "operating profit" vary between analysts; always state yours.
  • No qualitative view — management quality, brand, regulation, competition are invisible.
  • Ratios are symptoms, not diagnoses: a falling NPM tells you to look; it doesn't tell you why.

The margin and leverage ratios on this page are two of the three drivers of return on equity:

DuPont identityROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier = (PAT / Revenue) × (Revenue / Avg Total Assets) × (Avg Total Assets / Avg Equity)

Tiffinwala FY26 (averages of the two balance sheets): 9.375% × 1.2698 × 1.6471 = 19.61%. Profitability (§3) and leverage (§4) both feed it; efficiency (Session 4) supplies the middle term.

Recap Compare a ratio with its own past, with peers, with the industry and with the budget. Know the limits: historical, policy-dependent, year-end snapshots, no qualitative view.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Ratios turn raw statement figures into comparable signals. Profitability ratios divide each profit level of the P&L — gross profit, EBITDA, EBIT, PAT — by revenue to show how much of each rupee of sales survives each layer of cost. Leverage ratios compare borrowings with owners' funds (D/E) and operating profit with interest (ICR) to show how much financial risk the company carries. Tiffinwala grew 25% but lost margin at the gross level and took on debt; its leverage is still comfortable. Read every ratio against its trend, its peers and its industry, and remember that debt that lifts ROE in good years deepens losses in bad ones.

Practice & self-check

Revenue ₹50,00,000; COGS ₹32,00,000; operating expenses ₹10,00,000 (including depreciation ₹2,00,000); interest ₹1,50,000; tax 25%. Compute GPM, EBITDA margin, OPM and NPM.

Gross profit = 50,00,000 − 32,00,000 = ₹18,00,000 → GPM = 18/50 × 100 = 36%.
EBIT = 18,00,000 − 10,00,000 = ₹8,00,000 → OPM = 8/50 × 100 = 16%. EBITDA = 8,00,000 + 2,00,000 = ₹10,00,000 → 20%.
PBT = 8,00,000 − 1,50,000 = ₹6,50,000; tax = ₹1,62,500; PAT = ₹4,87,500 → NPM = 4.875/50 × 100 = 9.75%.

A firm's gross profit margin is 30% on revenue of ₹1,20,00,000. What is its cost of goods sold? If its NPM is 8%, what is PAT?

Gross profit = 30% × 1,20,00,000 = ₹36,00,000 → COGS = 1,20,00,000 − 36,00,000 = ₹84,00,000. PAT = 8% × 1,20,00,000 = ₹9,60,000.

Share capital ₹40,00,000; reserves & surplus ₹20,00,000; term loan ₹30,00,000; debentures ₹15,00,000; trade payables ₹10,00,000. Compute D/E both ways.

Equity = 40,00,000 + 20,00,000 = ₹60,00,000. Interest-bearing debt = 30,00,000 + 15,00,000 = ₹45,00,000 → D/E = 45/60 = 0.75. Total liabilities = 45,00,000 + 10,00,000 = ₹55,00,000 → D/E = 55/60 = 0.92. Trade payables are a liability but not debt.

EBIT is ₹24,00,000 and interest is ₹8,00,000. Find ICR. What happens to ICR if EBIT falls 40%?

ICR = 24,00,000 / 8,00,000 = 3.00x. New EBIT = 0.60 × 24,00,000 = ₹14,40,000 → ICR = 14,40,000 / 8,00,000 = 1.80x — into the "watch" zone; interest stays fixed while EBIT falls.

Total assets ₹1,00,00,000, EBIT ₹20,00,000, tax 25%. Compare ROE (PAT/equity) if (a) all equity, (b) 50% debt at 10%.

(a) PAT = 0.75 × 20,00,000 = ₹15,00,000; equity ₹1,00,00,000 → ROE = 15%.
(b) Interest = 10% × 50,00,000 = ₹5,00,000; PBT = ₹15,00,000; PAT = ₹11,25,000; equity ₹50,00,000 → ROE = 22.5%. Leverage lifts ROE because assets earn 20% (EBIT/assets) while debt costs 10%.

PAT ₹30,00,000; depreciation ₹10,00,000; interest ₹12,00,000; principal instalment due ₹20,00,000. Compute DSCR and comment.

DSCR = (30,00,000 + 10,00,000 + 12,00,000) / (12,00,000 + 20,00,000) = 52,00,000 / 32,00,000 = 1.63x (1.625). Above the 1.25–1.5x banks usually expect — the loan is serviceable.

A product costs ₹80 and sells for ₹100. State the gross margin and the markup.

Margin = 20/100 = 20% (on price). Markup = 20/80 = 25% (on cost). Ratio analysis always uses margin.

Concept: why can two companies with the same NPM have very different risk?

NPM ignores how the business is financed and how stable its EBIT is. A firm with high D/E and low interest cover can show the same NPM today but swing to a loss if EBIT falls, because interest is fixed. Check leverage (D/E, ICR) alongside margins.
