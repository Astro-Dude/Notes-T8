1 Meaning of options & the vocabulary

Explain like I'm 5

You pay a flat owner a ₹50,000 token to hold the price at ₹80 lakh for 3 months. If prices jump, you buy at ₹80 lakh. If they crash, you walk away and lose only the token. That token deal is an option; the token is the premium.

  • An option is a derivative contract that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed price on (or before) a fixed date.
  • The buyer pays a premium up front. The seller (writer) keeps the premium and takes on the obligation to perform if the buyer exercises.
  • Two types: call option = right to buy; put option = right to sell.
  • Contrast with futures (Session 14): in a future both sides are obliged. An option is asymmetric: the buyer's loss is capped at the premium.

Worked example — buying one Nifty call

Given: Nifty spot 24,000. You buy 1 lot of NIFTY 24000 CE at ₹390; lot size 75 (illustrative).

Cost: 390 × 75 = ₹29,250 paid once, up front. Notional controlled: 24,000 × 75 = ₹18,00,000.

At expiry the call pays max(Nifty − 24,000, 0) per unit; profit = payoff − 390.

Reading the table: below 24,000 you don't exercise, so you lose exactly the ₹29,250 premium, never more. Breakeven = 24,000 + 390 = 24,390. Every point above that is ₹75 profit per lot.

Common mistakes

(1) Forgetting to multiply the per-unit premium by the lot size. (2) Saying the seller has a right — the writer only has an obligation (and gets the premium for it). (3) Treating "premium" as the strike: premium is the option's price; strike is the price of the underlying deal.

Recap Option = right (buyer) vs obligation (writer). Call → buy, put → sell, at strike K by expiry T. Buyer pays premium × lot; that is the most a buyer can lose.

2 Calls vs puts: the four positions

Explain like I'm 5

A call is a pre-booking for a new phone at today's price — great if the price goes up. A put is insurance on your bike — the insurer must "buy" the loss from you if its value crashes.

Worked example — all four positions on one stock

Given: Masala Motors Ltd. (fictional) trades at ₹1,000. 1-month options, strike ₹1,000: call ₹40, put ₹35. Lot = 500 shares (illustrative).

Line by line: at ₹1,100 the call is worth exercising (buy at 1,000, worth 1,100 → payoff 100) and the put is not. At ₹900 the put pays 100 (sell at 1,000 something worth 900) and the call lapses.

Interpretation: long + short of the same option always sum to zero — options are a zero-sum game between buyer and writer (before brokerage and taxes).

Common mistakes

Writing a put is bullish, not bearish: the writer wins if the price stays up and must buy at K if it falls. "Short" refers to the option, not to the underlying.

Recap Call payoff max(ST − K, 0), put payoff max(K − ST, 0). Buyers: loss capped at premium. Writers: gain capped at premium, losses large (unlimited for a short call).

3 American vs European options

Explain like I'm 5

A European option is a movie ticket for the Sunday 7 pm show only. An American option is a pass you can use at any show until Sunday. The flexible pass can never be worth less.

FeatureAmerican optionEuropean option
When can it be exercised?Any day up to and including expiryOnly on the expiry date
Value≥ European (extra right to exercise early)≤ American
PricingBinomial trees / numerical methodsBlack–Scholes formula works; put–call parity holds exactly
Writer's riskCan be assigned any dayAssigned only at expiry
Where you see itMost US single-stock optionsMost index options (e.g. S&P 500 SPX); all NSE index and stock options (hence CE/PE)
Can you exit before expiry?Yes — exercise or sellYes — sell (square off) in the market; just can't exercise

The names are about exercise style, not geography. A third style, Bermudan, allows exercise on a few set dates.

Worked example — why early exercise usually loses money

Given: American call, K = ₹1,200, two months to expiry. Stock at ₹1,260. Call trades at ₹85. No dividend due.

  • Exercise now: pay 1,200, get a share worth 1,260 → gain 1,260 − 1,200 = ₹60.
  • Sell the call instead: receive ₹85.
  • Difference: 85 − 60 = ₹25 = the time value you throw away by exercising.

Interpretation: an American call on a non-dividend stock should never be exercised early, so it is worth the same as the European one. Early exercise can make sense for (a) a call just before a large dividend, or (b) a deep in-the-money put (getting K now earns interest).

Key takeaway

American ≥ European. For Indian exchange-traded options the question doesn't arise: they are European, and you exit early by selling, not exercising.

Recap American: exercise any time; European: only at expiry. Selling beats exercising while time value > 0. NSE options are European.

4 Moneyness, intrinsic & time value

Explain like I'm 5

A ₹500-off coupon on a ₹2,000 jacket is worth ₹500 if you use it today (intrinsic value). If it is valid for another month, it is worth a bit more — the jacket might go on sale and the coupon might stack (time value).

  • Moneyness = would the option pay if exercised right now?
  • Intrinsic value = that immediate exercise value (never negative).
  • Time value = premium − intrinsic value = the price of the chance that things get better before expiry. It shrinks to 0 at expiry (time decay, theta).
MoneynessCallPutIntrinsic value
In the money (ITM)S > KS < K> 0
At the money (ATM)S ≈ KS ≈ K0
Out of the money (OTM)S < KS > K0 (premium is all time value)
Premium splitPremium = Intrinsic value + Time value · IVcall = max(S − K, 0) · IVput = max(K − S, 0)

Common mistakes

(1) Negative intrinsic value — impossible; OTM means IV = 0. (2) "ITM = profitable": an ITM call bought at ₹85 with IV ₹60 still shows a loss if it expires at IV ₹60. (3) Swapping the put rule: a put is ITM when S is below K.

Recap ITM/ATM/OTM compares S with K (opposite for puts). Premium = IV + TV; TV is highest ATM and decays to 0 at expiry, fastest near the end.

5 What drives the premium: parity & binomial

Explain like I'm 5

Bike insurance costs more for a longer policy and a riskier rider. Option premiums work the same way: more time and more volatility mean more chances of a big payout, so a higher price.

Six factors (what happens when each one rises, others fixed)

Factor ↑Call premiumPut premiumWhy
Spot price S↑↓Call pays S − K; put pays K − S
Strike K↓↑Higher price to buy (bad for call), to sell at (good for put)
Time to expiry T↑↑ (usually)More time for a big move; American always ↑; a deep-ITM European put can be an exception
Volatility σ↑↑Downside is capped at the premium, upside isn't — bigger swings help the buyer
Risk-free rate r↑↓PV of K (paid later by a call buyer / received later by a put buyer) falls
Dividends expected↓↑Share price drops by the dividend on ex-date

Indian context: r is usually proxied by a T-bill / G-Sec yield (e.g. 7%); σ is read from the market as implied volatility (India VIX is the Nifty's 30-day implied vol).

Put–call parity (European options, no dividends)

Put–call parityC + K·e−rT = P + S0

Why it holds: both portfolios pay exactly max(ST, K) at expiry, so they must cost the same today.

Worked example — find the put, then spot an arbitrage

Given: Dosa Digital Ltd. (fictional) S0 = ₹1,500; 3-month (T = 0.25) European options, K = ₹1,500; r = 7% (0.07) continuous; call = ₹88.

Step 1 — PV of strike: e−0.07×0.25 = e−0.0175 = 0.982652 → K·e−rT = 1,500 × 0.982652 = ₹1,473.98.

Step 2 — parity: P = C + K·e−rT − S0 = 88 + 1,473.98 − 1,500 = ₹61.98.

Step 3 — the market quotes the put at ₹70 (too expensive by 70 − 61.98 = ₹8.02). Sell the expensive side (P + S), buy the cheap side (C + PV(K)):

Interpretation: ₹8.02 per share today, nothing owed later, whatever the price — a riskless profit. Arbitrageurs doing this push the put price back towards ₹61.98.

With a known dividend D before expiry: C + K·e−rT = P + S0 − PV(D).

One-step binomial model

Assume the stock can only go to one of two prices by expiry. Build a portfolio of shares + borrowing that copies the option's payoff; the option must cost what that portfolio costs (no arbitrage).

Hedge ratio (delta)Δ = (Cu − Cd) / (Su − Sd)
Risk-neutral probability & valuep = (1 + r − d) / (u − d) · C0 = [p·Cu + (1 − p)·Cd] / (1 + r)
SymbolMeaning
u, dUp and down factors (Su = u·S0, Sd = d·S0)
Cu, CdOption payoff in the up / down state
rRisk-free rate for the period (decimal)
pRisk-neutral probability of "up" — not the real-world probability
Worked example — pricing a call on Chaiwala Ltd. (fictional)

Given: S0 = ₹100; in 6 months it is either ₹120 (u = 1.2) or ₹80 (d = 0.8). K = ₹100. Risk-free rate 4% for the 6 months (≈ 8% a year).

                     up ×1.2    S_u = 120    C_u = max(120 - 100, 0) = 20
                   /
 S_0 = 100, C = ?
                   \
                     down ×0.8  S_d = 80     C_d = max(80 - 100, 0)  = 0

Method 1 — replicate: Δ = (20 − 0)/(120 − 80) = 0.5 share. In the down state 0.5 share is worth 40, so borrow PV(40) = 40/1.04 = ₹38.46 today. Check: up → 0.5 × 120 − 40 = 20 ✓; down → 0.5 × 80 − 40 = 0 ✓. Cost = 0.5 × 100 − 38.46 = ₹11.54.

Method 2 — risk-neutral: p = (1.04 − 0.8)/(1.2 − 0.8) = 0.24/0.40 = 0.6 → C0 = (0.6 × 20 + 0.4 × 0)/1.04 = 12/1.04 = ₹11.54.

The put (K = 100): Pu = 0, Pd = 20 → P0 = (0.4 × 20)/1.04 = ₹7.69. Parity check (discrete): C + K/(1+r) = 11.54 + 96.15 = 107.69 = P + S = 7.69 + 100 ✓.

Interpretation: the option's value doesn't depend on anyone's guess of the real up-probability — only on the two prices and r. Δ = 0.5 is also the hedge ratio: 0.5 share per call written makes the position riskless (§6).

Black–Scholes (European, shown not derived)

Black–ScholesC = S0·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2) · P = K·e−rT·N(−d2) − S0·N(−d1)
d1 = [ln(S0/K) + (r + σ2/2)T] / (σ√T) · d2 = d1 − σ√T

N(·) = standard normal cumulative probability. Plugging in Dosa Digital (S = K = 1,500, r = 7%, T = 0.25) with σ = 25% (0.25):

  • Row 7: d2 = 0.2025 − 0.125 = 0.0775. N(d1) = 0.5802, N(d2) = 0.5309.
  • Row 8: 1,500 × 0.5802 − 1,473.98 × 0.5309 = 870.30 − 782.54 = 87.76 with 4-dp N values; ₹87.84 at full precision — the "₹88" call used above.

Common mistakes

(1) Using 7 instead of 0.07, or months instead of years for T. (2) Mixing continuous e−rT with a discrete per-period rate — pick one and stay with it. (3) Applying parity to American options (it becomes an inequality). (4) Treating p in the binomial model as the true chance of "up".

Recap S↑, r↑ help calls; K↑, dividends↑ help puts; T↑ and σ↑ help both. C + K·e−rT = P + S. Binomial: Δ = ΔC/ΔS, p = (1+r−d)/(u−d), discount the risk-neutral expected payoff.

6 Option strategy — hedging

Explain like I'm 5

Buying a put on shares you own is like buying insurance on your scooter: a small premium each year, and if there's a crash someone else pays for most of the damage.

  • Hedging = taking a position that gains when your existing exposure loses, to cap the damage.
  • Options hedge one side only: you lose the premium but keep the good outcomes. Futures/forwards lock the price both ways at no premium.

Worked example 2 — insuring a ₹45 lakh portfolio with Nifty puts

Given: Portfolio ₹45,00,000, beta 1.2 (moves 1.2 × Nifty). Nifty 24,000, lot 75 (illustrative). Buy the 23,500 put at ₹105 (from the chain in §4).

Lots: 1.2 × 45,00,000 / (24,000 × 75) = 54,00,000 / 18,00,000 = 3 lots. Cost: 3 × 75 × 105 = ₹23,625 (0.525% of the portfolio).

Hedge ratio / delta (intuition)

DeltaΔ = change in option price / change in underlying price · call: 0 to +1 · put: −1 to 0 · ATM ≈ ±0.5
Delta hedgeOptions needed = Number of shares / |Δ|
Worked example 4 — delta-neutral protection

500 shares; a put with Δ = −0.4. Puts needed = 500 / 0.4 = 1,250 puts = 2.5 lots of 500. Check for a ₹10 fall: shares lose 500 × 10 = ₹5,000; puts gain 0.4 × 10 × 1,250 = ₹5,000 → net ≈ 0.

Delta changes as the price moves and time passes, so a delta hedge must be rebalanced. The binomial Δ = 0.5 in §5 is the same idea.

Common mistakes

(1) Hedging a β 1.2 portfolio with lots based on value alone (ignoring β) → under-hedged. (2) Ignoring basis / tracking risk: a real portfolio won't move exactly 1.2 × Nifty. (3) Forgetting the premium in the hedged P&L. (4) Picking a far-OTM put because it's cheap — the deductible (S − K) gets bigger.

Recap Long exposure → buy puts; short exposure → buy calls. Max loss with a protective put = (S0 − K) + premium. Lots = β × V / (index × lot). Futures lock both ways; options insure one way for a premium.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

An option splits risk unevenly: the buyer pays a premium for a right, the writer takes the obligation for that premium. Calls pay above the strike, puts below it; whether an option is ITM, ATM or OTM decides its intrinsic value, and the rest of the premium is time value that melts to zero by expiry. Premiums rise with time and volatility, and calls and puts are tied together by put–call parity — a no-arbitrage argument the binomial model extends to pricing. European options (all NSE options) can only be exercised at expiry, but can always be sold. Used as a hedge, a bought put puts a floor under a portfolio and a bought call caps a future cost, for a known premium, while keeping the upside that a futures hedge gives away.

Practice & self-check

A stock trades at ₹540. The ₹500 call costs ₹52. Moneyness, intrinsic value and time value?

S > K → ITM. IV = 540 − 500 = ₹40. TV = 52 − 40 = ₹12.

You buy a ₹300 put at ₹18 (lot 1,000, illustrative). At expiry the stock is ₹260. Your profit, and the writer's?

Payoff = max(300 − 260, 0) = 40. Profit = 40 − 18 = ₹22/share × 1,000 = ₹22,000. Writer: 18 − 40 = −₹22/share = −₹22,000 (zero-sum). Breakeven was 300 − 18 = ₹282.

S = ₹200, K = ₹210, r = 8% continuous, T = 6 months, European put = ₹15. What should the call cost?

K·e−rT = 210 × e−0.04 = 210 × 0.960789 = 201.77. C = P + S − K·e−rT = 15 + 200 − 201.77 = ₹13.23.

One-step binomial: S0 = ₹50, goes to ₹60 or ₹40; K = ₹50; r = 5% per period. Price the call two ways.

u = 1.2, d = 0.8, Cu = 10, Cd = 0. Risk-neutral: p = (1.05 − 0.8)/0.4 = 0.625 → C = 0.625 × 10 / 1.05 = ₹5.95. Replication: Δ = 10/20 = 0.5; borrow 20/1.05 = 19.05; C = 0.5 × 50 − 19.05 = ₹5.95 ✓. (Put = 0.375 × 10/1.05 = ₹3.57; parity: 5.95 + 50/1.05 = 53.57 = 3.57 + 50 ✓.)

You hold 1,000 shares bought at ₹250 and buy the ₹240 put at ₹6. Max loss, breakeven, and P&L if the stock ends at ₹200 or ₹280?

Max loss = (250 − 240) + 6 = ₹16/share = ₹16,000. BE = 250 + 6 = ₹256. At ₹200: −50 + 40 − 6 = −16 → −₹16,000. At ₹280: +30 − 6 = 24 → +₹24,000.

A ₹80 lakh portfolio has β = 0.9. Nifty is 24,000, lot size 75 (illustrative). How many Nifty put lots for a full hedge?

0.9 × 80,00,000 / (24,000 × 75) = 72,00,000 / 18,00,000 = 4 lots.

Why is an American option worth at least as much as a European one, and why is an American call on a non-dividend stock still never exercised early?

It has every right the European has plus early exercise, so it can't be worth less. Exercising early gives only intrinsic value; selling the call gets intrinsic + time value (e.g. ₹85 vs ₹60 in §3), so selling always wins without a dividend.

Volatility jumps and the stock price rises at the same time. What happens to a call's premium? A put's?

Call: both effects push it up → rises. Put: σ↑ pushes up, S↑ pushes down → net effect depends on which is bigger (can't say without numbers).
