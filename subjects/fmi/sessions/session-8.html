1 Capital budgeting: meaning, decisions & process

Explain like I'm 5

A chai-stall owner wonders whether a ₹2 lakh coffee machine is worth it. Capital budgeting asks: will the extra cash it brings over the next 5 years — counted in today's rupees — beat the ₹2 lakh paid today?

  • Capital budgeting: planning and evaluating large, long-term investments — new machinery, a new product line, acquiring a business — whose cash flows extend many years into the future.
  • Why it matters (slide):
    • Large sums committed up front.
    • Largely irreversible once made (the money becomes a sunk cost).
    • Shapes the firm's cash flows, risk and competitiveness for years or decades.
    • One wrong capital budgeting decision can destroy far more value than an operating mistake.
Type of decisionMeaningExample
ExpansionAdd capacity to grow the businessTata Motors setting up a new EV plant at Sanand, Gujarat (slide)
ReplacementReplace worn-out or outdated assetsSwapping old diesel delivery vans for an EV fleet
DiversificationEnter new products/markets to spread riskReliance Industries entering retail (slide)
Independent projectsAccepting one doesn't affect the others; take every one that passesA new warehouse and a new website
Mutually exclusive projectsOnly one can be chosenHotel chain: rooftop solar or LED retrofit (§5)
Contingent projectsOne only makes sense if another is acceptedA packing line that needs the new plant first

Recap Capital budgeting = evaluating big, long-lived, irreversible investments. Decisions: expansion, replacement, diversification; independent vs mutually exclusive. Process: identify → estimate → evaluate → decide → monitor.

2 Relevant (incremental) cash flows

Explain like I'm 5

Count only the money that changes because you said "yes" to the project. The ₹500 you already spent on a survey is gone whether you say yes or no — ignore it.

  • Appraisal uses incremental cash flows: firm cash flow with the project − without it. Cash, not accounting profit; after tax.

Recap Use incremental, after-tax cash flows. Ignore sunk costs and interest; include opportunity costs, working capital (out then back) and after-tax salvage. OCF = (R − C)(1 − t) + tD.

3 Net Present Value (NPV)

Explain like I'm 5

₹100 next year is worth less than ₹100 today. NPV converts every future rupee a project earns into today's rupees, then subtracts what you pay today. What's left is your gain.

  • NPV discounts every future cash flow back to today at the firm's cost of capital, then deducts the initial investment — the value a project adds, in today's rupees, over and above the return investors already require.
  • NPV of ₹8,00,000 = the project is expected to make shareholders ₹8 lakh richer in present-day terms.
Net Present Value (slide, expanded)NPV = −I0 + CF1/(1 + r)1 + CF2/(1 + r)2 + … + CFn/(1 + r)n
Net Present Value (slide, Σ form)NPV = Σt=1..n CFt ÷ (1 + r)t − I0
Equal annual cash flows (annuity shortcut)NPV = CF × PVIFA(r, n) − I0,   PVIFA = [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r
SymbolMeaning (slide)
I0Initial investment (at t = 0)
CF1, CF2, … CFnFuture (incremental) cash flows; CFt = cash flow in period t
rRequired rate of return = cost of capital (decimal: 12% → 0.12)
nProject life
ConditionDecisionInterpretation
NPV > 0AcceptEarns more than the cost of capital; creates value
NPV = 0IndifferentEarns exactly the required return; value unchanged
NPV < 0RejectEarns less than the cost of capital; destroys value
  • Mutually exclusive: pick the single highest positive NPV.
  • Value additivity: NPVs of independent projects simply add up.

Worked example 1 — Bharat AutoParts (slide)

4 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

Explain like I'm 5

If the project were a bank FD, what interest rate would it be paying you? That rate is its IRR. Take it if it beats what your money costs you.

  • IRR: the discount rate at which NPV = 0 — the project's own break-even rate of return (compound annual return on the money invested).
  • No algebraic shortcut in general → trial-and-error, interpolation, or a calculator / spreadsheet =IRR().
  • Popular in Indian corporate and banking practice: one percentage, comparable to an FD or loan rate.
IRR definition (slide)0 = Σt=1..n CFt ÷ (1 + IRR)t − Initial investment
Linear interpolation (slide)IRR = L + [NPVL ÷ (NPVL − NPVH)] × (H − L)
SymbolMeaning
L, HLower and higher trial discount rates
NPVLNPV at the lower rate (positive)
NPVHNPV at the higher rate (negative) — so NPVL − NPVH adds the two magnitudes
SituationDecision rule
Single, independent projectAccept if IRR > cost of capital (hurdle rate); reject if IRR < cost of capital
Mutually exclusive projectsPrefer the higher IRR only if it agrees with NPV; if rankings conflict, follow NPV
Unconventional cash flowsCheck for multiple IRRs (signs change more than once); if found, rely on NPV
Capital rationingIRR alone isn't enough — use PI or total NPV of feasible combinations

Trial-and-error method

  1. Pick a starting rate. Equal cash flows: compute the target factor PVIFA = investment ÷ annual CF and find the rates in the table that bracket it.
  2. Compute NPV at a rate that gives NPV > 0 (L) and one that gives NPV < 0 (H). Keep them 1–2 percentage points apart.
  3. Interpolate. Check: NPV > 0 at the cost of capital ⇔ IRR > cost of capital (conventional flows).
Worked example 1 — Bharat AutoParts (slide), corrected
RatePVIFA(r, 5)PV of inflows (₹)NPV (₹)

Interpolating 16%–17%: IRR = 16% + [1,85,734 ÷ (1,85,734 + 1,635)] × 1% = 16% + 0.99% = 16.99% ≈ 17%.

Annuity shortcut: target PVIFA = 80,00,000 ÷ 25,00,000 = 3.2000, between 3.2743 (16%) and 3.1993 (17%) → 16% + (3.2743 − 3.2000) ÷ (3.2743 − 3.1993) × 1% = 16.99%.

Decision: 16.99% > 12% → accept, consistent with NPV > 0.

Slide note: at 17% the slide shows PV ₹80,02,000 and NPV +₹2,000; the correct figures are ₹79,98,365 and −₹1,635 (IRR is just below 17%). Interpolating over a wide gap (12%–18%) gives 17.09% — linear interpolation overstates IRR on a curved NPV profile, so keep L and H close.

MIRR — fixing the reinvestment assumption (beyond the slides)

Modified IRRMIRR = (FV of inflows reinvested at r ÷ PV of outflows)1/n − 1

Bharat AutoParts: FV of inflows at 12% = 25,00,000 × FVIFA(12%, 5) = 25,00,000 × 6.3528 = ₹1,58,82,118 → MIRR = (1,58,82,118 ÷ 80,00,000)1/5 − 1 = 14.70%. Lower than the 16.99% IRR because interim cash is assumed to earn 12%, not 16.99%. Excel: =MIRR(B2:B7, 12%, 12%).

Common mistakes
  • Interpolating between two rates whose NPVs have the same sign — you must bracket zero.
  • Forgetting the minus sign: NPVL − NPVH = 1,85,734 − (−1,635) = 1,87,369 (add the magnitudes).
  • Using IRR to choose between mutually exclusive projects without checking NPV (§5).
Recap IRR is the r that makes NPV = 0. Find two rates bracketing zero, interpolate: L + NPVL/(NPVL − NPVH) × (H − L). Accept if IRR > cost of capital. Excel =IRR(CF0:CFn).

5 When NPV and IRR disagree

Explain like I'm 5

Would you rather earn 50% on ₹100 (gain ₹50) or 20% on ₹1,00,000 (gain ₹20,000)? The higher percentage is not the bigger gain. NPV counts the rupees.

Worked example — slide: a hotel chain's dilemma (mutually exclusive, r = 10%)

Why they disagree (slide + homework)

ReasonWhat happens
1. Scale differencesIRR is a % and ignores investment size; a small project can post a high IRR yet add little value (hotel A vs B).
2. Reinvestment assumptionIRR assumes interim cash is reinvested at the IRR itself; NPV assumes the cost of capital (more realistic).
3. Multiple IRRsUnconventional cash flows (e.g. big decommissioning cost at the end) can give more than one IRR.
4. Timing differencesSame size, but one project's cash comes early and the other's late (example below).

6 Payback & discounted payback

Explain like I'm 5

How many months of extra sales until the new coffee machine has "paid for itself"? That's payback — popular with shop owners because it fits in one sentence.

  • Payback period: time for cumulative cash inflows to recover the initial investment — a test of liquidity and risk exposure ("how fast?", not "how much?").
  • Discounted payback: same, but each cash flow is first discounted at the cost of capital. Always ≥ simple payback.
  • Rule: set a cut-off (e.g. 4 years). Accept if payback ≤ cut-off; reject if longer (or, for discounted payback, if it is never reached within the life).
Payback (slide)Payback = Years before full recovery + Remaining amount ÷ Next year's cash flow
Equal annual cash flowsPayback = Initial investment ÷ Annual cash flow
Discounted paybackSame formula using discounted cash flows CFt ÷ (1 + r)t

Fraction of a year × 12 = months (0.2 yr = 2.4 months).

7 Capital rationing, PI & the full appraisal sheet

Explain like I'm 5

With ₹500 pocket money and many snacks to choose from, you pick the most taste per rupee — unless the best treat only comes in one big ₹500 pack.

  • Capital rationing: the total budget is smaller than the investment needed for every positive-NPV project.
  • Soft rationing: a self-imposed ceiling set by management for planning/control. Hard rationing: an external limit — lenders/markets won't supply more funds.
  • Under rationing, the highest-NPV project may not be optimal: look at value created per rupee invested.
Profitability Index (slide)PI = PV of future cash flows ÷ Initial investment = 1 + NPV ÷ Initial investment
  • PI > 1 ⇔ NPV > 0 → accept. PI = 1 ⇔ NPV = 0. PI < 1 → reject.
  • Rationing: rank by PI (highest first) and fund down the list until the budget runs out — valid when projects are divisible.
Worked example — slide: rationing a ₹1 crore budget (independent projects, r = 10%)
ProjectInvestment (₹)PV of inflows (₹)NPV (₹)PIPI rank

  • By PI alone: B (₹10 lakh) then C (₹40 lakh) = ₹50 lakh used, total NPV = 4,26,439 + 5,48,960 = ₹9,75,399; A (₹1 crore, indivisible) no longer fits, ₹50 lakh sits idle.
  • A alone: uses the full ₹1 crore, NPV = ₹21,94,730 — more than B + C.

Decision: fund A. PI ranking suits small, divisible projects; for "lumpy" (indivisible) ones, compare the total NPV of every feasible combination.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Capital budgeting is Session 7's time value of money applied to a project. First get the incremental, after-tax cash flows right (no sunk costs, no interest, include opportunity costs and working capital). Then NPV tells you how many rupees of value the project adds at the cost of capital; IRR is the rate at which that value is zero; PI is value per rupee for rationed budgets; payback and discounted payback measure how fast the cash comes back. When the methods disagree — scale, timing, multiple IRRs, lumpy budgets — total NPV is the tie-breaker.

Practice & self-check

A project costs ₹5,00,000 and returns ₹2,00,000, ₹2,50,000 and ₹1,50,000 over 3 years. Cost of capital 10%. Find the NPV and decide.

PVs: 2,00,000 ÷ 1.10 = 1,81,818; 2,50,000 ÷ 1.21 = 2,06,612; 1,50,000 ÷ 1.331 = 1,12,697 → total 5,01,127. NPV = 5,01,127 − 5,00,000 = +₹1,127 → accept (barely; IRR ≈ 10.13%, just above 10%).

Investment ₹10,00,000; ₹3,00,000 a year for 5 years. Find the IRR by interpolation.

Target PVIFA = 10 ÷ 3 = 3.3333. PVIFA(15%, 5) = 3.352155 → NPV = 10,05,647 − 10,00,000 = +5,647. PVIFA(16%, 5) = 3.274294 → NPV = 9,82,288 − 10,00,000 = −17,712. IRR = 15% + 5,647 ÷ (5,647 + 17,712) × 1% = 15.24%.

Cash flows: (6,00,000); 1,50,000; 2,00,000; 2,50,000; 3,00,000. r = 10%. Find payback and discounted payback.

Cumulative: 1,50,000; 3,50,000; 6,00,000 → payback = 3.00 years. Discounted CFs: 1,36,364; 1,65,289; 1,87,829; 2,04,904 → cumulative 1,36,364; 3,01,653; 4,89,482; 6,94,386. Discounted payback = 3 + (6,00,000 − 4,89,482) ÷ 2,04,904 = 3 + 0.54 = 3.54 years. (NPV = +₹94,386.)

Bharat AutoParts: find its PI and show it gives the same decision as NPV.

PI = PV of inflows ÷ investment = 90,11,941 ÷ 80,00,000 = 1.13 > 1 → accept. Equivalent: PI = 1 + NPV/I = 1 + 10,11,941/80,00,000 = 1.13; PI > 1 exactly when NPV > 0.

Mutually exclusive: L has NPV ₹12 lakh, IRR 18%; S has NPV ₹5 lakh, IRR 30%. Cost of capital 12%. Which do you choose and why do the rankings differ?

L — it adds ₹7 lakh more value. The conflict comes from scale (and/or timing): IRR is a % and ignores size, and assumes reinvestment at 30%/18% rather than 12%. For mutually exclusive projects, NPV decides.

A project's cash flows are −, +, +, − (big decommissioning cost at the end). What problem can IRR have, and what should you do?

Two sign changes → up to two IRRs (or none), so "IRR > cost of capital" is ambiguous. Compute NPV at the cost of capital (or MIRR) and decide on that.

Which are relevant cash flows for a new plant? (a) ₹2 lakh consultancy study already paid (b) a vacant warehouse that could be let for ₹3 lakh/yr (c) interest on the loan funding the plant (d) ₹5 lakh extra inventory (e) depreciation.

(a) No — sunk. (b) Yes — opportunity cost (after tax). (c) No — financing is in the discount rate. (d) Yes — working-capital outflow now, recovered at the end. (e) Not as a cash flow, but its tax shield t × D is included.

A colleague types =NPV(10%, B1:B6) where B1 is the year-0 outlay of −₹10,00,000. What's wrong?

=NPV treats B1 as an end-of-year-1 amount, so every cash flow is discounted one extra year: the result = true NPV ÷ 1.10. Fix: =NPV(10%, B2:B6) + B1.
