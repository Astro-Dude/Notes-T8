1 Why money has time value

Explain like I'm 5

₹10,000 today can sit in a bank FD and become ₹10,700 next year — and the chai that costs ₹15 today may cost ₹17 next year. So ₹10,000 today beats ₹10,000 next year, every time.

  • Time value of money (TVM): a rupee today is worth more than a rupee in the future, because today's rupee can be invested to earn a return.
  • It is the engine of the firm's investment decision (slide "Corporate Finance — Big Picture", after Damodaran): invest only in assets that earn more than the hurdle rate, where the return must reflect the magnitude and timing of cash flows.
Decision (goal: maximise firm value)Question it answersWhere we study it
InvestmentWhich assets/projects earn more than the hurdle rate? (magnitude + timing of cash flows)S7 TVM → S8 NPV/IRR
FinancingWhat mix of debt and equity? Debt tenor should match the assets' life.S9 cost of capital

The three classroom situations

SituationChoiceFactsBest pick & why
1₹10,000 today or ₹10,000 in 1 yearPhone costs ₹10,000 now and laterToday — same money, earlier; you can buy the phone now or invest it.
2₹10,000 today or ₹11,000 in 1 yearYou can earn 10% p.a.Indifferent — 10,000 × 1.10 = ₹11,000. Both are worth the same.
3₹10,000 today or ₹11,000 in 1 yearInterest 10%, but phone price rises 20% to ₹12,000Today — ₹11,000 next year can't buy a ₹12,000 phone. Real return = 1.10 ÷ 1.20 − 1 = −8.33%.

Factors behind the time value of money

  • Consumption preference — most people prefer consuming now over later; to wait, they must be paid.
  • Investment opportunity — money today can be invested to earn returns and grow.
  • Inflation — purchasing power of money falls over time.
  • Opportunity cost — every rupee locked in one use gives up the return of the next-best use.
  • Risk (standard addition) — a promised future payment may not arrive in full.

2 Future value & compounding

Explain like I'm 5

Compounding is a snowball: your FD earns interest, and next year that interest also earns interest.

  • Compounding moves value forward in time: PV → FV.
  • One period at rate r: ₹1 becomes ₹(1 + r). ₹100 at 10% → ₹110 = principal ₹100 + interest ₹10.
  • n periods: multiply by (1 + r) n times. (1 + r)n is the future value interest factor (FVIF).
Future value of a single cash flowFV = PV × (1 + r)n

Worked example — slide: student deposit

Given: PV = ₹10,000, r = 10% (0.10), n = 3, compounded annually.

FV = 10,000 × (1.10)3 = 10,000 × 1.331 = ₹13,310.

The deposit grows by ₹3,310 purely through compounding. Slide check: ₹10,000 at 7.5% for 5 years = 10,000 × 1.4356 = ₹14,356.29.

Worked example — slide: the power of compounding

Great-grandfather invests ₹100 in 1900 at 10% p.a. Value 120 years later (2020)?

FV = 100 × (1.10)120 = 100 × 92,709.07 = ₹92,70,906.88 (≈ ₹92.7 lakh).

Note: the slide states ₹100 but multiplies ₹10 × 92,709.07 = ₹9,27,090.69. For ₹100 the answer is ₹92.7 lakh.

Worked example — slide: credit card at 2% a month (APR vs EAR)

Given: purchase ₹100, rate 2% per month (0.02), n = 12 months.

FV = 100 × (1.02)12 = 100 × 1.2682 = ₹126.82 owed after 1 year.

APR (nominal) = 2% × 12 = 24%. Effective annual rate = (126.82 − 100) ÷ 100 = 26.82%.

The slide labels 26.82% as "APR"; strictly, 24% is the APR (nominal) and 26.82% is the EAR — the true annual cost.

Worked example — bank FD, quarterly compounding

Given: ₹1,00,000 FD, 7% p.a. compounded quarterly, 5 years. r/m = 7% ÷ 4 = 1.75% (0.0175); m × n = 20.

FV = 1,00,000 × (1.0175)20 = 1,00,000 × 1.4148 = ₹1,41,477.82.

EAR = (1.0175)4 − 1 = 7.19%. Banks quote 7%; you actually earn 7.19% a year.

Recap FV = PV(1 + r)n; compound interest earns interest on interest. With m compoundings a year use r/m and m × n; EAR = (1 + r/m)m − 1. Rule of 72 for doubling time.

3 Present value & discounting

Explain like I'm 5

You need ₹10,000 for a trip in 5 years. Discounting tells you how much to put in the piggy bank (FD) today so it grows into exactly ₹10,000.

  • Discounting moves value backward in time: FV → PV. It is compounding solved for PV.
  • 1 ÷ (1 + r)n is the discounting factor (present value factor, PVIF, DF).
  • Higher r or longer n → smaller PV.
Present value of a single cash flowPV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n = FV × 1/(1 + r)n
CompoundingDiscounting
DirectionForward: PV (today) → FV (future)Backward: FV (future) → PV (today)
FormulaFV = PV × (1 + r)nPV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n
Factor(1 + r)n — compounding factor (FVIF), ≥ 11 ÷ (1 + r)n — discounting factor (PVIF), ≤ 1
Typical question"What will my SIP/FD be worth?""What is a future receipt worth today?"

Worked example — saving for an MBA

Given: you need ₹20,00,000 in 6 years; an FD/debt fund earns 9% p.a.

DF = 1 ÷ (1.09)6 = 1 ÷ 1.6771 = 0.5963 → PV = 20,00,000 × 0.596267 = ₹11,92,534.65.

Depositing about ₹11.93 lakh today covers the ₹20 lakh fee.

Solving for the rate (CAGR) or the time

Rate (CAGR)r = (FV ÷ PV)1/n − 1
Number of periodsn = ln(FV ÷ PV) ÷ ln(1 + r)
Worked example — CAGR and time to target

CAGR: a share bought at ₹500 is worth ₹1,000 after 6 years. r = (1,000 ÷ 500)1/6 − 1 = 20.1667 − 1 = 12.25% p.a. (Rule of 72 check: 72 ÷ 6 = 12%.)

Time: how long for ₹2,00,000 to become ₹5,00,000 at 9%? n = ln(2.5) ÷ ln(1.09) = 0.9163 ÷ 0.0862 = 10.63 years.

Recap PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n. Discount factor 1/(1 + r)n shrinks with r and n. Rearrange for CAGR or time; in Excel use =PV, =RATE, =NPER.

4 Multiple (uneven) cash flows

Explain like I'm 5

A kirana shop's extra sales from a new freezer differ every year. Bring each year's money back to today separately, then add — like converting different currencies to rupees before adding.

  • Real projects rarely pay a single amount. When cash flows differ each period, value each one separately with its own t and sum.
  • Used for equipment cash-flow schedules, project appraisal (S8) and irregular loan repayments.
PV of multiple cash flowsPV = Σt=1..n CFt ÷ (1 + r)t
FV of multiple cash flows (at year n)FVn = Σt=1..n CFt × (1 + r)n − t = PV × (1 + r)n

  • FV column: each CF compounded to year 4, e.g. year-1 CF grows 3 years: 5,000 × 1.331 = 6,655.

Worked example — pay cash or take the instalment plan?

Laptop: ₹1,00,000 cash, or ₹40,000 now + ₹35,000 after 1 year + ₹35,000 after 2 years. Your money earns 10% elsewhere.

PV(plan) = 40,000 + 35,000 ÷ 1.10 + 35,000 ÷ 1.21 = 40,000 + 31,818.18 + 28,925.62 = ₹1,00,743.80.

Plan costs ₹743.80 more in today's money → pay cash. At 12%, PV(plan) = 40,000 + 31,250.00 + 27,901.79 = ₹99,151.79 → the plan wins. The answer depends on your opportunity cost.

  • Adding undiscounted cash flows (₹26,000) — rupees at different dates are different "currencies".
  • Putting a t = 0 amount inside =NPV() — Excel then discounts it one year.
Recap Uneven stream → discount each CF by its own (1 + r)t and add. FV = PV × (1 + r)n. =NPV starts at year 1.

5 Annuities — ordinary, due & growing

Explain like I'm 5

A ₹10,000 SIP every month, or the same rent every month, is an annuity — equal amounts at equal gaps. One formula replaces adding up 120 separate discounted payments.

Worked example — slide: graduate invests ₹10,000 at the end of every year for 5 years at 10%

PV = 10,000 × [1 − (1.10)−5] ÷ 0.10 = 10,000 × [1 − 0.6209] ÷ 0.10 = 10,000 × 3.7908 = ₹37,908 (₹37,907.87) — lump sum today equal to the 5 deposits.

FV = 10,000 × [(1.10)5 − 1] ÷ 0.10 = 10,000 × 6.1051 = ₹61,051 — fund value at the end of year 5.

Worked example — insurance premium: lump sum or yearly in advance?

Pay ₹1,00,000 now for a 5-year policy, or ₹24,000 at the start of each year for 5 years. r = 8%.

PVIFA(8%, 5) = 3.9927 → due factor = 3.9927 × 1.08 = 4.3121 → PV = 24,000 × 4.3121 = ₹1,03,491.04.

Check via "first payment now + 4-year ordinary annuity": 24,000 + 24,000 × 3.3121 = ₹1,03,491.04. Instalments cost ₹3,491 more in PV → pay the lump sum.

Worked example — monthly SIP (annuity due)

₹10,000 at the start of every month for 10 years; assumed return 12% p.a. → r = 1% per month (0.01), n = 120.

FVIFA(1%, 120) = [(1.01)120 − 1] ÷ 0.01 = (3.3004 − 1) ÷ 0.01 = 230.0387.

FVdue = 10,000 × 230.0387 × 1.01 = ₹23,23,390.76. You invest ₹12,00,000; compounding adds ≈ ₹11.23 lakh. (Mutual-fund returns are not guaranteed; 12% is illustrative.)

Growing annuity

PV of a growing annuity (first payment C1 at t = 1, growing at g)PV = C1 ÷ (r − g) × [1 − ((1 + g) ÷ (1 + r))n]
Worked example — rent that rises 5% a year

A shop is let for 10 years. Rent: ₹3,00,000 at the end of year 1, rising 5% a year. r = 10%.

((1.05) ÷ (1.10))10 = 0.6280 → PV = 3,00,000 ÷ 0.05 × (1 − 0.6280) = 60,00,000 × 0.3720 = ₹22,31,943.64.

A flat ₹3,00,000 for 10 years is worth only 3,00,000 × 6.1446 = ₹18,43,370.13 — the 5% escalation adds ≈ ₹3.9 lakh of value.

Common mistakes
  • Wrong period: annual rate with monthly payments. Always match r and n to the payment frequency.
  • Valuing rent/premiums/SIPs (paid in advance) as an ordinary annuity — understates value by a factor (1 + r).
  • Counting n wrongly: 5 payments → n = 5, whatever the timing.
Recap PVIFA = [1 − (1 + r)−n]/r, FVIFA = [(1 + r)n − 1]/r. Annuity due = ordinary × (1 + r). Growing annuity: C1/(r − g) × [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + r))n].

6 Loan EMI & amortisation

Explain like I'm 5

Buying a phone on EMI: each month's instalment first pays that month's interest; whatever is left chips away at the loan. Early EMIs are mostly interest, later ones mostly principal.

  • An EMI (equated monthly instalment) loan is an ordinary annuity: the loan amount is the PV of all EMIs at the monthly rate.
  • Indian bank loans charge interest on the reducing (outstanding) balance.
  • An amortisation schedule splits every EMI into interest and principal.
EMI (annuity formula solved for A)EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1] = P ÷ PVIFA(r, n)

rMonthly rate = annual rate ÷ 12 (12% p.a. → 1% = 0.01)
nNumber of monthly instalments (years × 12)

Common mistake — "flat rate" is not the real rate

A dealer offers the ₹60,000 phone at "12% flat": interest = 60,000 × 12% × 1 = ₹7,200 → EMI = 67,200 ÷ 12 = ₹5,600. Flat interest is charged on the original amount even as you repay it. True reducing-balance rate: =RATE(12, -5600, 60000) = 1.79% a month = 21.46% p.a. (EAR 23.70%) — almost double the quoted 12%.

Recap EMI = P × r(1 + r)n/[(1 + r)n − 1] with monthly r and n. Each row: interest = opening × r, principal = EMI − interest. Longer tenure → smaller EMI, far more interest.

7 Perpetuity & terminal value

Explain like I'm 5

A college wants a ₹2 lakh scholarship every year, forever. Put ₹25 lakh in an 8% bond: the interest alone (₹2 lakh) pays it each year and the ₹25 lakh is never touched.

  • Perpetuity: an annuity that continues forever, with no maturity date.
  • Growing perpetuity (Gordon model): first payment CF1, growing at g forever; needs r > g.
Constant (level) perpetuityPV = CF ÷ r
Growing perpetuity (Gordon), r > gPV = CF1 ÷ (r − g)
Worked example — slide: preference share paying ₹500 a year

Constant: ₹500 every year forever, r = 10% → PV = 500 ÷ 0.10 = ₹5,000.

Growing: first payment ₹500, growing 4% forever, r = 10% → PV = 500 ÷ (0.10 − 0.04) = 500 ÷ 0.06 = ₹8,333.33.

Growth of just 4% a year raises the value by two-thirds.

Worked example — deferred perpetuity (exam twist)

₹50,000 a year forever, first payment at the end of year 4, r = 10%.

Step 1: the perpetuity formula values the stream one period before its first payment → value at t = 3 = 50,000 ÷ 0.10 = ₹5,00,000.

Step 2: discount 3 years → PV = 5,00,000 ÷ (1.10)3 = 5,00,000 ÷ 1.331 = ₹3,75,657.40.

Terminal value

  • In project/business valuation, cash flows are forecast explicitly for a few years (n). Terminal value (TV) captures the value of all cash flows beyond year n, assuming stable growth forever.
  • TV is a value as at year n, so it must itself be discounted back n years.
Terminal value (Gordon growth) at end of year nTVn = CFn × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g)
Present value of terminal valuePV(TV) = TVn ÷ (1 + r)n
Exit-multiple method (alternative)TVn = year-n metric (cash flow / EBITDA) × chosen multiple
SymbolMeaning
CFnLast explicitly forecast cash flow (year n); CFn(1 + g) = first cash flow of the perpetuity
gLong-term stable growth rate (must be < r; usually ≤ long-run economic growth)
rDiscount rate
Worked example — slide: project forecast ends in year 5 with ₹2,00,000; g = 3%; r = 12%

Step 1 — TV at year 5: TV5 = 2,00,000 × 1.03 ÷ (0.12 − 0.03) = 2,06,000 ÷ 0.09 = ₹22,88,889.

Step 2 — discount to today: PV = 22,88,889 ÷ (1.12)5 = 22,88,889 ÷ 1.7623 = ₹12,98,777.

The slide shows ₹12,98,590 — an arithmetic slip; 22,88,888.89 ÷ 1.762342 = ₹12,98,777.03.

Common mistakes
  • Using CFn instead of CFn(1 + g) in the numerator (Gordon needs next year's cash flow).
  • Forgetting to discount TV, or discounting it by (1 + r)n+1.
  • Applying Gordon when g ≥ r — the formula gives a negative or infinite value; it is invalid.
Recap Perpetuity CF/r; growing CF1/(r − g). Terminal value = CFn(1 + g)/(r − g) at year n, then ÷ (1 + r)n. TV often dominates total value — the bridge to DCF in Session 10.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Rupees at different dates are different currencies; (1 + r)n is the exchange rate. Compounding carries a value forward, discounting brings it back, and every cash-flow pattern is just a shortcut for adding many discounted amounts: an uneven stream is added term by term, an annuity collapses into PVIFA/FVIFA, an EMI is that annuity solved for the payment, and a perpetuity or terminal value is an annuity that never ends. Session 8 uses exactly these tools to decide whether a project's future cash flows are worth today's investment.

Practice & self-check

₹25,000 is invested at 8% p.a. for 4 years. Find the FV with (a) annual and (b) quarterly compounding.

(a) 25,000 × (1.08)4 = 25,000 × 1.3605 = ₹34,012.22. (b) r/m = 2%, mn = 16: 25,000 × (1.02)16 = 25,000 × 1.3728 = ₹34,319.64. Quarterly compounding earns ₹307.42 more.

What is ₹5,00,000 receivable after 7 years worth today at 10%?

DF = 1 ÷ (1.10)7 = 1 ÷ 1.9487 = 0.5132. PV = 5,00,000 × 0.513158 = ₹2,56,579.06.

A credit card charges 1.5% per month. State its APR and its effective annual rate.

APR = 1.5% × 12 = 18%. EAR = (1.015)12 − 1 = 1.1956 − 1 = 19.56% — the true annual cost of carrying a balance.

Car loan ₹3,00,000 at 10% p.a. for 3 years, monthly EMIs. Find the EMI and total interest.

r = 10% ÷ 12 = 0.8333%, n = 36, (1 + r)36 = 1.3482. EMI = 3,00,000 × 0.008333 × 1.3482 ÷ 0.3482 = ₹9,680.16 (=PMT(10%/12, 36, -300000)). Total interest = 9,680.16 × 36 − 3,00,000 ≈ ₹48,486.

Office rent of ₹50,000 a year for 3 years, r = 9%. Find the PV if rent is paid (a) at the end of each year, (b) in advance. Why does (b) cost more?

PVIFA(9%, 3) = 2.5313. (a) Ordinary: 50,000 × 2.5313 = ₹1,26,564.73. (b) Due: 1,26,564.73 × 1.09 = ₹1,37,955.56. Each payment in (b) is made one year earlier, so it is discounted one year less.

(a) A preference share pays ₹8 a year forever; r = 10%. (b) A share's next dividend is ₹5, growing 5% forever; r = 12%. Value each.

(a) Perpetuity: 8 ÷ 0.10 = ₹80. (b) Growing perpetuity: 5 ÷ (0.12 − 0.05) = 5 ÷ 0.07 = ₹71.43. ₹5 is already D1, so don't multiply by 1.05 again.

A project's year-3 cash flow is ₹10,00,000; after that it grows 4% forever. r = 11%. Find TV3 and its present value.

TV3 = 10,00,000 × 1.04 ÷ (0.11 − 0.04) = 10,40,000 ÷ 0.07 = ₹1,48,57,143. PV = 1,48,57,143 ÷ (1.11)3 = 1,48,57,143 ÷ 1.3676 = ₹1,08,63,415.

What monthly SIP (end of month) builds ₹1 crore in 15 years at 12% p.a.?

r = 1%, n = 180. FVIFA(1%, 180) = [(1.01)180 − 1] ÷ 0.01 = 499.58. SIP = 1,00,00,000 ÷ 499.58 = ₹20,016.81 a month (=PMT(1%, 180, 0, -10000000)).
