1 Meaning, objectives & users of accounting

Explain like I'm 5

The kirana uncle's notebook (bahi-khata) where he writes every sale, every purchase and who owes him money — that notebook, done properly and summed up at year-end, is accounting.

Definition (AICPA — the one in the slides)

"Accounting is the art of recording, classifying and summarizing, in a significant manner and in terms of money, transactions and events which are, in part at least, of a financial character, and interpreting the results thereof."

  • Called the language of business: every activity — buying stock, paying salaries, collecting from customers, borrowing — has a money effect, and accounting turns those effects into decision-ready information.
  • Warren Buffett (slide quote): unless you can read and interpret financial statements, "you really shouldn't select stocks yourself."

The process (Session 2 recap slide adds identifying, analysing and communicating):

Worked example — "Cash went up, so we made a profit?" (the slides' retail-store question)

Given (Sharma General Store, April): bank balance rose from ₹2,00,000 to ₹2,60,000 (+₹60,000). Cash sales ₹3,00,000. Goods bought and paid ₹2,40,000, of which goods costing ₹40,000 are still on the shelf. Salaries ₹30,000, rent & utilities ₹20,000. The owner also put in ₹50,000 of extra capital.

Recap Accounting = record, classify, summarise in money terms, then interpret. Seven objectives; users split into internal (management, employees) and external (investors, lenders, suppliers, government, public). Cash going up ≠ profit.

3 Elements of accounting

Explain like I'm 5

Your chai stall's kettle is an asset, the ₹5,000 you owe the milkman is a liability, what's left for you is equity. Selling chai is income; buying milk for it is an expense; selling your old stove for more than it's worth on paper is a gain.

LiabilityObligation of the business to an external party — what it owesBank loan, trade payables, salaries payable, tax payable—
EquityOwners' residual claim after liabilities are deducted from assetsCapital, share capital, retained earnings, reservesIt is equity

Assets and liabilities: current vs non-current

Current (within the operating cycle / ~12 months)Non-current (beyond 12 months)
AssetsCash, bank balance, accounts receivable, inventory, short-term investments, prepaid expensesBuildings, machinery, computers, vehicles, long-term investments, certain intangibles, right-of-use (lease) assets
LiabilitiesAccounts payable, salaries payable, interest payable, taxes payable, short-term borrowingsLong-term bank loans, long-term bonds, certain lease liabilities

Equity: where it comes from, what reduces it

  • Increased by: capital invested by owners, profits retained, additional owner contributions.
  • Reduced by: losses, drawings (owner takes cash/goods for personal use) or dividends (company pays shareholders).
Equity — two business formsProprietorship: Equity = Owner's capital + Profit − Drawings
Company: Equity = Share capital + Retained earnings + Other reserves

Gain or loss on selling an asset

Gain / (loss) on disposalGain (Loss) = Sale proceeds − Carrying amount   ·   Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation

GreenBite Café (slide): fridge with carrying amount ₹70,000 sold for ₹90,000 → gain ₹20,000. Coffee machine carrying ₹45,000 sold for ₹30,000 → loss ₹15,000.

Common mistakes
  • Calling an advance received from a customer "income". Until you deliver, it is a liability.
  • Calling the whole sale price of an old asset a gain. Gain = proceeds − carrying amount only.
  • Treating drawings or dividends as expenses. They reduce equity directly; they are not in the P&L.
Recap Seven elements: assets, liabilities, equity (balance-sheet items) and income, expenses, gains, losses (which change equity). Current = within ~12 months. Gain/loss = proceeds − carrying amount.

4 The accounting equation

Explain like I'm 5

You buy a ₹60,000 phone: ₹20,000 from your savings, ₹40,000 on EMI. The phone (asset) = what the bank is owed (liability) + your share (equity). Every rupee the business owns was funded by someone.

Accounting equationAssets = Liabilities + Equity   ⇔   Equity = Assets − Liabilities
  • Foundation of double-entry accounting (dual aspect, §5): every transaction has at least two effects.
  • The equation must balance after every transaction — if it doesn't, you've made a mistake.
Expanded equation (proprietorship)Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses − Drawings
TermMeaningMoves equity
CapitalMoney/assets the owner invested+
Income, gainsEarned from main business / incidental+
Expenses, lossesUsed up to earn income / incidental−
Drawings (dividends for a company)Owner takes value out−

The slide writes "Equity = Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses". Read it as: the part of equity created during the period (the period's profit) = Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses. Total equity also includes capital and is reduced by drawings.

Proprietorship vs company equity

ItemProprietorship (Rahul's Cafe)Company (TechStar Ltd.)

Common mistakes
  • Changing only one side: a credit sale raises assets (receivables) and equity (income) — not cash.
  • Putting drawings or dividends in the P&L as an expense. They reduce equity, not profit.
  • Thinking "equity = cash". Equity is a claim on all assets minus liabilities.
Recap A = L + E always. Income and gains raise equity; expenses, losses and drawings/dividends cut it. Check every transaction: change in assets = change in liabilities + change in equity.

5 Accounting concepts & conventions

Explain like I'm 5

Cricket needs fixed rules so every match's score means the same thing. Concepts and conventions are accounting's rules, so Reliance's profit and a kirana's profit are measured the same way.

They guide how transactions are recorded, classified, measured and reported, so statements are consistent, comparable, reliable, understandable and useful for decisions. Concepts = the basic assumptions; conventions = accepted practices for applying them.

The 9 accounting concepts (slides)

ConceptRuleExample
Business entityBusiness is separate from its ownerAmazon's books exclude Jeff Bezos's personal spending; owner paying home electricity from the business account = drawings

Historical costAssets recorded initially at acquisition cost, not current market valueLand bought for ₹50 lakh stays at ₹50 lakh even if now worth ₹2 crore
Dual aspectEvery transaction has at least two effects; basis of A = L + ELoan received: cash ↑ and loan liability ↑
AccrualRecord when the economic activity occurs, not when cash movesMarch ad campaign paid in April is a March expense

Worked example — Accrual (slides: ABC Ltd.)

Digital ad campaign run in March 2026; agency charges ₹1,00,000; ABC pays in April 2026.

The service was received in March → ₹1,00,000 is a March 2026 expense (FY 2025-26). On 31 March it also shows ₹1,00,000 as a liability (expense payable). April's payment just settles the liability.

Sales ₹60,000, of which ₹10,000 is credit to a nearby office (collected in April). Milk, tea, sugar used ₹20,000 (paid in cash). Rent ₹24,000 paid on 1 March for March–May. March electricity ₹5,000 paid in April.

Buys 1,000 shirts at ₹1,000; sells 600 at ₹1,800.

Worked example — Revenue recognition (slides: Netflix; own: gym)
  • Netflix: you pay ₹12,000 on 1 April 2025 for 12 months. Netflix recognises ₹12,000 / 12 = ₹1,000 revenue per month as it provides the service; the unearned part is a liability (contract liability / income received in advance).
  • Gym (own): annual membership ₹24,000 paid on 1 January 2026. FY 2025-26 ends 31 March 2026 → 3 months earned = 24,000 × 3/12 = ₹6,000 revenue; the other ₹18,000 is a liability on 31 March 2026.

The 4 accounting conventions (slides)

ConventionRuleExample
ConsistencyOnce a method is adopted, keep using it unless there's a valid reason to changeAB Ltd can't switch depreciation methods every year to manage profit

Worked example — Prudence (own)

Closing stock cost ₹2,00,000. Case 1: it can now be sold for only ₹1,70,000 (net realisable value). Case 2: it can be sold for ₹2,40,000.

  • Case 1: value at ₹1,70,000 → recognise the expected loss of 2,00,000 − 1,70,000 = ₹30,000 now.
  • Case 2: keep at cost ₹2,00,000 → the ₹40,000 gain is recognised only when the goods are sold.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing accrual and matching: accrual = record when it happens; matching = put the expense in the same period as the revenue it earned. Matching is applied through accrual.
  • Recognising an upfront annual fee as revenue on day 1 (revenue recognition is per period of service).
  • Using market value for a building "because it's more realistic" — historical cost is the starting rule.
Recap 9 concepts: entity, going concern, money measurement, period, historical cost, dual aspect, accrual, matching, realisation. 4 conventions: consistency, prudence, full disclosure, materiality. Each needs a one-line example in exams.

6 Transaction analysis

Explain like I'm 5

A see-saw that must stay level: whatever you add on one side, you add on the other side too — or take something off the same side.

Four-step method

  1. Which elements are affected? (cash, receivable, loan, income…)
  2. Does each go up or down?
  3. By how much? (for disposals: proceeds vs carrying amount)
  4. Check: ΔAssets = ΔLiabilities + ΔEquity.
PatternExampleEquation still balances because…
Asset ↑, asset ↓Buy equipment for cash; debtor paysTotal assets unchanged
Asset ↑, liability ↑Bank loan; goods bought on credit; advance from customerBoth sides up
Asset ↑, equity ↑Owner invests; sale (income)Both sides up
Asset ↓, liability ↓Pay a supplier; repay a loanBoth sides down
Asset ↓, equity ↓Pay rent (expense); drawings; dividend; depreciationBoth sides down
Liability ↑, equity ↓Salary or interest due but unpaidRight side shifts internally

Common mistakes
  • Recording the full sale price as equity on a sale. Equity rises by sale − cost of goods sold (₹60,000 in row 5, not ₹2,10,000), because inventory also falls.
  • Counting revenue again when a debtor pays (row 8). It was counted when the sale happened.
  • Forgetting month-end accruals (row 12) — profit gets overstated.
Recap For each transaction: elements → up/down → amount → check ΔA = ΔL + ΔE. A running table makes errors visible immediately; its final row is the balance sheet, and the equity changes are the P&L.

★ Putting it all together

The big idea

Accounting records a business's transactions in money and turns them into information for internal and external users. Everything recorded is one of seven elements, and the accounting equation A = L + E ties them together: income and gains raise equity, expenses, losses and drawings reduce it. Concepts such as entity, accrual, matching and revenue recognition decide when and how much to record, so profit reflects what was earned, not just cash moved. Analyse each transaction with ΔA = ΔL + ΔE, and the final balances become the balance sheet and P&L of Session 2.

Practice & self-check

Opening capital ₹4,00,000. During the year the owner added ₹1,00,000 and withdrew ₹60,000. Closing capital is ₹5,20,000. What was the profit?

Profit = Closing − Opening − Additional + Drawings = 5,20,000 − 4,00,000 − 1,00,000 + 60,000 = ₹80,000. (Check: 4,00,000 + 1,00,000 + 80,000 − 60,000 = 5,20,000.)

A business has assets of ₹22,00,000 and equity of ₹14,50,000. What are its liabilities? It then buys machinery for ₹3,00,000 on credit — new totals?

L = 22,00,000 − 14,50,000 = ₹7,50,000. After the purchase: machinery +3,00,000 (A) and creditor +3,00,000 (L) → A ₹25,00,000 = L ₹10,50,000 + E ₹14,50,000.

An asset costing ₹3,00,000 with accumulated depreciation of ₹1,80,000 is sold for ₹1,00,000 cash. Show the effect on the equation.

Carrying amount = 3,00,000 − 1,80,000 = ₹1,20,000. Loss = 1,00,000 − 1,20,000 = (₹20,000). Cash +1,00,000, asset −1,20,000 → ΔA = −20,000; ΔE = −20,000 (loss); ΔL = 0. Balanced.

A phone retailer buys 500 phones at ₹8,000 and sells 350 at ₹11,000. Revenue, COGS, gross profit and closing inventory?

Revenue = 350 × 11,000 = ₹38,50,000. COGS = 350 × 8,000 = ₹28,00,000. Gross profit = ₹10,50,000. Closing inventory = 150 × 8,000 = ₹12,00,000 (asset). Matching concept.

An EdTech firm receives ₹36,000 on 1 February 2026 for a 6-month course. How much revenue in FY 2025-26, and what appears on the 31 March 2026 balance sheet?

Monthly = 36,000 ÷ 6 = ₹6,000. February + March = ₹12,000 revenue. The remaining ₹24,000 is a current liability (income received in advance / contract liability). Revenue recognition concept.

The owner pays his child's school fees of ₹40,000 from the business bank account. Which concept applies and how is it recorded?

Business entity concept — it's not a business expense. Record as drawings: cash −40,000, equity −40,000. Profit is unaffected.

Name the concept or convention: (a) closing stock valued at ₹1,70,000 NRV instead of ₹2,00,000 cost; (b) land shown at ₹50 lakh purchase price though worth ₹2 crore; (c) a ₹300 calculator expensed immediately; (d) March salaries paid in April charged to March.

(a) Prudence / conservatism · (b) Historical cost · (c) Materiality · (d) Accrual (and matching).

Salary of ₹30,000 for March is unpaid on 31 March. Which elements change and how?

Expense ₹30,000 → equity −30,000; salaries payable (current liability) +30,000. Assets unchanged: 0 = +30,000 − 30,000. When paid in April: cash −30,000 and salaries payable −30,000.
